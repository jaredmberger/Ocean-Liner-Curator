How Long Did It Take Titanic to Sink?

Titanic did not disappear immediately after striking the iceberg. The disaster unfolded over roughly two hours and forty minutes, moving from damage assessment to evacuation, structural failure, and the final plunge.

RMS Titanic April 14–15, 1912 Sinking timeline
Short answer

Titanic took about 2 hours and 40 minutes to sink. She struck the iceberg at about 11:40 p.m. ship’s time on April 14, 1912, and disappeared beneath the surface at about 2:20 a.m. on April 15.

The basic timeline

The familiar answer is simple: Titanic struck the iceberg shortly before midnight and sank in the early hours of the next morning. But the full timeline matters because the sinking was not one single moment. It was a sequence of flooding, assessment, distress calls, lifeboat launching, increasing trim, structural failure, and final disappearance.

The approximate time between collision and sinking is usually given as 2 hours and 40 minutes. That figure comes from the commonly accepted collision time of about 11:40 p.m. and the final sinking time of about 2:20 a.m., both in Titanic’s ship time.

Key times in the sinking

The exact minute assigned to some events can vary slightly depending on the source, testimony, and later reconstruction. The outline below gives a careful, practical sequence rather than pretending every second is known with modern precision.

About 11:40 p.m.
Titanic strikes the iceberg. The damage is not a single dramatic gash, but flooding begins in multiple forward compartments.
Shortly after midnight
The seriousness of the damage becomes clearer. Distress calls are sent, and preparations for evacuation begin.
About 12:25 a.m.
Lifeboat loading begins in earnest. Many early boats leave with empty seats because danger is still not fully understood by many passengers.
After 1:00 a.m.
The ship’s forward list and trim become more apparent. The urgency increases as water continues to move through the vessel.
After 2:00 a.m.
The final boats and collapsibles become central to the evacuation. The ship’s angle steepens and the remaining time is very short.
About 2:20 a.m.
Titanic sinks. The final minutes include structural failure and the stern’s disappearance beneath the surface.

Why did Titanic stay afloat that long?

Titanic remained afloat for hours because the damage did not instantly destroy the ship’s buoyancy. The flooding began forward and progressed through compartments over time. The ship’s watertight subdivision slowed the disaster, but it could not stop it once too many forward compartments were open to the sea.

In practical terms, Titanic was mortally damaged quickly but did not sink quickly. That distinction is important. The collision made the loss likely; the sinking itself took time because water had to enter, spread, change the ship’s trim, and eventually overcome the hull’s remaining buoyancy.

Curator’s note: The phrase “Titanic sank in 2 hours and 40 minutes” is useful, but it compresses a complicated event. A stronger interpretation separates the moment of fatal damage from the long, uneven process of the ship actually going under.

Why do some times vary slightly?

Small differences in Titanic timelines are normal. Witnesses experienced the sinking from different locations, under extreme stress, and without the kind of synchronized timekeeping expected in a modern incident report. Later reconstructions also have to reconcile testimony, wireless records, ship time, lifeboat accounts, and physical evidence.

That is why responsible Titanic timelines often use words like about, approximately, or shortly after. The broad sequence is well established, but some individual moments should not be overstated with false precision.

What happened in the final minutes?

By the final stage, Titanic’s bow was deeply down, the stern was rising, and the ship’s structure was under immense stress. The breakup did not occur at the moment of collision; it happened near the end, after flooding had already forced the ship into an extreme condition.

The final disappearance was therefore much faster than the whole sinking process. For most of the night, Titanic’s decline was gradual enough for evacuation efforts to continue. In the last minutes, the situation changed rapidly.

The best short answer

For a quick answer, say Titanic took about 2 hours and 40 minutes to sink. For a more careful answer, add that the collision occurred at about 11:40 p.m. on April 14, 1912, and the ship sank at about 2:20 a.m. on April 15.

That framing gives the essential fact without implying the entire disaster unfolded at one constant speed. Titanic’s sinking was a timed sequence, not a single instant.
