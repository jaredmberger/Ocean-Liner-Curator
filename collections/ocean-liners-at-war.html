Ocean Liners at War

A thematic collection on how civilian passenger liners were transformed by the world wars—as troopships, hospital ships, exchange vessels, military transports, targets, survivors, and ships forced to rebuild an identity after conflict.

Research Collections Ship Archive Liner History
Collection Type War, Service & Transformation
Core Period 1914–1947
Primary Context World Wars I & II
Collection Scope Military conversion, loss, neutrality, transport, and postwar restoration

Ocean liners were civilian machines built around schedules, passengers, mail, cargo, and national prestige. War repeatedly broke that system apart. Large passenger ships offered exactly what governments suddenly needed: speed, range, accommodation, communications, medical space, and the ability to move thousands of people across oceans.

The result was not one single wartime story. Some liners became troopships; some served as hospital ships; some were seized from enemy owners; some remained neutral and carried diplomats, internees, and civilians in exchange voyages; some were destroyed before they could return to passenger service. Others survived, but came home physically and historically altered.

Curator’s Note

Interpretive note: wartime service should be treated as a distinct phase in a liner’s biography. Passenger capacities, interiors, paint schemes, ownership, legal status, routes, and even the name carried by a ship could change during conflict. A wartime photograph or document should therefore not be assumed to describe the vessel’s peacetime configuration.

How War Changed the Liner

Five recurring transformations provide the framework for this collection.

Troop Transport at ScaleSpeed, capacity, range

Large liners could move formations and replacement personnel across oceans far faster than ordinary transports, making passenger-ship capacity strategically valuable.

Hospital and Medical ServiceCivilian design repurposed

Passenger accommodation could be converted for medical use, but the new role created a ship whose spaces, markings, staffing, and operating context differed fundamentally from civilian service.

Seizure, Reflagging, and New IdentityOwnership transformed

War could turn an enemy liner into a new national asset. Vaterland’s transformation into Leviathan is among the clearest examples of conflict rewriting a ship’s identity.

Neutrality and Exchange VoyagesA different wartime role

Not every liner became a troopship. Neutral vessels could occupy a delicate diplomatic space, carrying exchanged civilians and other protected passengers between belligerent worlds.

Return to Civilian LifeRestoration and reinvention

Demobilization did not simply restore the prewar ship. Extensive refits, changed passenger markets, corporate changes, and wartime wear often created a materially different postwar vessel.

The First World War

The 1914–1918 conflict demonstrated how quickly the civilian liner network could become part of a military transport system.

RMS OlympicWhite Star Line · Troop transport

A prestige liner whose long career included extensive First World War military service before she returned to the Atlantic passenger trade.
RMS AquitaniaCunard · Two world wars

Aquitania’s unusually long life makes her especially valuable for comparing civilian service with military transport roles across both world wars.
HMHS BritannicWhite Star · Hospital ship

War intervened before the third Olympic-class liner could enter passenger service. Completed for medical service, she was lost in the Aegean in 1916.
RMS LusitaniaCunard · Wartime civilian loss

Lusitania illustrates a different problem: a commercial liner continuing to operate during wartime and becoming inseparable from the wider political meaning of maritime loss.
SS LeviathanFormer Vaterland · U.S. troop transport

The German liner Vaterland was seized by the United States in 1917 and transformed into Leviathan, one of the most important American troop transports of the conflict.
RMS MauretaniaCunard · Wartime conversion

The prewar speed symbol became part of the wartime transport and medical system, showing how express-liner performance could acquire strategic value.

The Second World War

By 1939, governments again drew heavily on passenger shipping—this time on a global scale and with many interwar flagships entering service histories radically unlike those for which they had been designed.

RMS Queen MaryCunard-White Star · Troopship

One of the defining wartime liner conversions: an Atlantic prestige flagship transformed into a high-capacity troop transport before an extensive return to civilian service.
RMS Queen ElizabethCunard-White Star · War before passengers

Her biography reverses the normal pattern: wartime transport service came before her delayed commercial passenger debut in 1946.
SS Nieuw AmsterdamHolland America · Troop transport

A 1938 flagship whose early civilian career was interrupted almost immediately, followed by wartime transport and a major postwar restoration.
MV GeorgicWhite Star · Bombed, salvaged, rebuilt

Georgic demonstrates that wartime survival could itself create a new ship biography: bombing, salvage, reconstruction, postwar trooping, and emigrant service.
RMS StrathnaverP&O · Long-distance troop service

A Britain–Australia liner redirected into military transport and later rebuilt for a very different postwar passenger economy.
MS GripsholmSwedish American Line · Neutral exchange voyages

Gripsholm broadens the collection beyond requisition: her wartime exchange voyages show the diplomatic and humanitarian roles available to a neutral passenger liner.

Ships That Did Not Simply Come Home

SS NormandieFrench Line · Wartime seizure and loss

A celebrated passenger liner immobilized by war, taken into U.S. custody, and lost during conversion—an extraordinary example of a peacetime identity overtaken by conflict.
SS BremenNorddeutscher Lloyd · Wartime loss

Her interwar express career ended with the outbreak of war; the ship was later destroyed by fire in Germany in 1941.
SS EuropaNorddeutscher Lloyd · Capture and rebirth

Europa survived the war but not her original identity: captured, used by the United States, and eventually rebuilt into the French liner Liberté.

A Wartime Liner Timeline

  • 1914: War fractures the international liner system. Ships are laid up, requisitioned, converted, rerouted, or exposed to new risks.
  • 1915–1916: Lusitania is lost in wartime commercial service; Britannic enters hospital-ship service and is later lost in the Aegean.
  • 1917: The United States seizes Vaterland at Hoboken; she becomes Leviathan and enters American transport service.
  • 1918–1920: Repatriation and demobilization create another enormous transport task before liners can be restored to civilian schedules.
  • 1939: A second global conflict again interrupts passenger networks and strands or requisitions major liners.
  • 1940: Queen Elizabeth leaves Britain before entering passenger service and begins the wartime chapter that will precede her commercial career.
  • 1941: Georgic is bombed at Port Tewfik and later salvaged; Bremen is destroyed by fire in Germany.
  • 1945–1947: Demobilization, repatriation, and reconstruction gradually return surviving liners to civilian service—but rarely in exactly their prewar form.
