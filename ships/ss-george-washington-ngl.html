SS George Washington

North German Lloyd · 1909 · Ship Guide

Overview

SS George Washington was a major North German Lloyd ocean liner built by AG Vulcan at Stettin and placed into Bremen–New York service in 1909. When new, she was among the largest liners in the world and was designed to emphasize passenger comfort and immigrant traffic rather than outright speed.

This guide focuses on the North German Lloyd liner while also summarizing the ship’s later U.S. Navy, United States Lines, and Army transport phases. Because the same hull served under several identities, dates and operator context are essential.

Design & Construction Context

George Washington was part of North German Lloyd’s strategy of serving the profitable emigrant and transatlantic passenger market with large, comfortable ships. Her American name was deliberate and commercially meaningful: it helped the ship appeal to passengers bound for the United States, especially emigrants for whom arrival in America carried symbolic weight.

She was not primarily a Blue Riband-style speed machine. Instead, her scale, accommodation, and public rooms emphasized a broad passenger business: first-class comfort, second-class respectability, and very large third-class capacity.

Service History (Summary)

George Washington began regular service in June 1909 from Bremen to New York via Southampton and Cherbourg. At the time, she was one of the largest liners afloat and one of the most prominent German-built passenger ships.

At the outbreak of World War I in 1914, the ship was interned in New York. When the United States entered the war in April 1917, she was seized and converted for American naval transport service as USS George Washington. In this role she carried troops and later became famous for carrying President Woodrow Wilson to Europe for the Paris Peace Conference.

After the war, the ship returned to civilian passenger work under American control, including service associated with the United States Shipping Board and United States Lines. By the early 1930s she had been laid up as transatlantic economics changed.

During World War II, the old liner was again pressed into transport service, briefly commissioned as USS Catlin and later operated as USAT George Washington. After the war she was laid up, damaged by fire in 1951, and then sold for scrapping.

Historical Significance

George Washington matters because she connects several major themes in ocean-liner history: German competition on the North Atlantic, large-scale immigrant transport, wartime seizure of enemy liners, presidential diplomacy, and the long afterlife of prewar passenger ships as military transports.

Her career is also a reminder that many liners did not have a single stable identity. A ship launched as a German passenger liner could become an American naval transport, a United States Lines passenger vessel, and an Army transport across two world wars.

Interpretive Notes

Because George Washington had several ownership and service phases, artifacts and printed material should be described by date and operator whenever possible. “North German Lloyd George Washington” material is not the same interpretive category as U.S. Navy, United States Lines, or Army transport material.

The strongest identifications will include route, company marks, voyage date, military designation, or documented provenance. Where a source simply says “George Washington,” researchers should check whether the item belongs to the German liner phase, the World War I transport phase, interwar passenger service, or World War II transport service.
