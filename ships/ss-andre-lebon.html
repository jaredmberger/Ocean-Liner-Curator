SS André Lebon (1915)

Messageries Maritimes · entered service 1915 · Ship Guide

Overview

SS André Lebon was a large Messageries Maritimes mail and passenger liner launched at La Ciotat in 1913 and completed during the First World War. Instead of beginning with ordinary commercial service, she immediately entered wartime use as a troop transport and later as a hospital ship before settling into the Marseille–Yokohama route after 1919.

Her career became unusually eventful even by liner standards. She sank in shallow water at Singapore in 1919 and was salvaged, sheltered more than 1,500 people during the 1923 Great Kantō earthquake at Yokohama, received survivors from the burning Georges Philippar in 1932, survived a second world war that included German seizure and an air attack at Toulon, and then returned yet again to Far East service before retirement in 1952.

War Before Peacetime

André Lebon entered service while the First World War was already underway. She was requisitioned almost immediately and first carried troops toward Salonika in December 1915. In 1916 she also moved Indochinese troops from Southeast Asia to France.

From September 1916 until 1918 she served as a hospital ship. In the final year of the war she also carried colonial and allied contingents toward Vladivostok as part of the Allied intervention in Siberia.

Marseille to Yokohama

After the war the ship finally settled into the civilian role for which she had been intended, operating between Marseille and Yokohama. The route took her through the Mediterranean, Suez, the Indian Ocean, Southeast Asia, China, and Japan.

Her mixed accommodation—three conventional passenger classes plus substantial lower-fare capacity—reflected the broad traffic of the Far East service rather than the narrow luxury market associated with prestige Atlantic liners.

The Singapore Sinking of 1919

While coaling at Singapore in 1919, André Lebon suddenly developed a heavy list as water entered through open ports. She sank in shallow water.

The accident might have ended the career of a newer ship, but the shallow depth made salvage possible. The liner was raised, repaired, and returned to service—an early example of the extraordinary survivability that would characterize the rest of her history.

The Great Kantō Earthquake

On 1 September 1923 André Lebon was at Yokohama when the Great Kantō earthquake devastated the region. Fires followed the earthquake and destroyed much of Yokohama.

Messageries Maritimes records state that the ship, despite undergoing boiler work, was maneuvered away from danger and took aboard more than 1,500 people seeking refuge. This episode became one of the defining humanitarian moments of her career.

Conversion from Coal to Oil

In 1924 the ship's boilers were converted from coal to oil firing. The change reduced dependence on large coaling operations and reflected the broader move toward oil fuel across major passenger fleets during the interwar years.

Externally the liner remained recognizably a prewar two-funnel steamship even as her machinery operation became more modern.

The Georges Philippar Rescue Chain

In May 1932 the new motor liner Georges Philippar caught fire in the Gulf of Aden. The Soviet tanker Sovietskaïa Neft rescued more than 400 survivors and subsequently rendezvoused with André Lebon.

The survivors were transferred to André Lebon, which diverted to Djibouti to land them. This makes the older ship directly part of the disaster history of her much newer fleetmate—not as the primary rescuing vessel, but as the crucial second link that carried hundreds of survivors safely to port.

Late Interwar Service

By the late 1930s André Lebon was already an elderly liner. Photographs from the Spanish Civil War period show large French neutrality markings on her hull, useful today for dating images from that era.

Despite her age, she remained valuable enough to be requisitioned again when war returned in 1939.

Second World War

During 1940 and 1941 the ship participated in the movement and repatriation of French troops from the Levant. By the end of 1941 she had been laid up at Marseille.

German forces seized her on 6 May 1943 and used the ship temporarily as a barracks. On 11 March 1944 American aircraft sank her in Toulon harbour.

Raised Again

For a second time in her life, sinking did not end the ship. André Lebon was raised in 1945 and repaired at La Seyne.

The decision to restore an already three-decade-old liner reflected the severe shortage of surviving French passenger tonnage after the war. Messageries Maritimes had lost much of its fleet and needed every usable hull.

Return to the Far East

The rebuilt ship resumed eastern service after the war. In September 1948 she grounded on a sandbank near Cape Cangio but was freed, adding yet another salvage incident to her already unusual history.

On 20 October 1948 she became the first Messageries Maritimes passenger liner to call at Yokohama after the war, symbolically reconnecting the company with a port deeply associated with her own 1923 history.

Final Years

André Lebon continued operating into the early 1950s, long after most ships of her generation had disappeared. Postwar accommodation was reorganized to carry 287 first-, 171 second-, and 107 third-class passengers.

She was finally sold for demolition at La Seyne in December 1952 after nearly forty years from launch and thirty-seven years from entry into service.

Why André Lebon Matters

André Lebon is a remarkable example of continuity across two world wars and the interwar period. Her history includes troop transport, hospital service, colonial passenger traffic, disaster relief, salvage, wartime seizure, bombing, postwar reconstruction, and long-distance civilian service.

She also ties together several stories already present in the archive: the Messageries Maritimes Far East network, the Georges Philippar disaster, and the company's difficult postwar reconstruction.

Collecting Profile

Surviving material spans an unusually wide range: prewar postcards, hospital-ship images, Far East passenger ephemera, Yokohama earthquake photographs, interwar menus, neutrality-marking photographs, wartime troopship views, and postwar publicity.

Because the ship changed role repeatedly while retaining the same name, context and date are essential. A photograph of André Lebon may document a hospital ship, a civilian liner, a troop transport, a damaged wartime hull, or a postwar survivor.

Interpretive Notes

She was not the primary rescuer of Georges Philippar: Sovietskaïa Neft picked up the large group later transferred to André Lebon.

She sank twice and returned twice: Singapore in 1919 and Toulon in 1944 were separate sinkings followed by successful salvage.

Her 1923 Yokohama role is unusually well documented: the more-than-1,500 figure comes from the Messageries Maritimes historical record and should remain tied to that source.
