MS St. Louis

Hamburg-America Line · entered service 1929 · Ship Guide

Overview

MS St. Louis was a Hamburg-America Line diesel passenger liner built by Bremer Vulkan at Bremen-Vegesack as yard no. 670. Launched on 2 August 1928 and entering service in March 1929, she normally worked from Hamburg to Halifax and New York and also made leisure cruises.

Her historical importance is dominated by the May–June 1939 refugee voyage. Carrying 937 passengers, most of them German Jews fleeing Nazi persecution, St. Louis was denied a safe haven in Cuba and the United States and ultimately returned to Europe. The episode has become one of the clearest symbols of the international failure to provide refuge before the Holocaust.

Correcting the Prefix

This ship is properly described as MS St. Louis, reflecting her diesel propulsion. The existing site path remains /ships/ss-st-louis for continuity, but the guide title and metadata now use the more accurate prefix.

The 1939 Voyage

On 13 May 1939 St. Louis left Hamburg for Havana with 937 passengers, most of them Jewish refugees. United States Holocaust Memorial Museum records show that when the ship reached Havana on 27 May, most passengers were not permitted to land. The ship then sailed near the United States while efforts to secure admission continued, but the passengers were not allowed to disembark there either.

The ship returned across the Atlantic. Through negotiations involving the American Jewish Joint Distribution Committee and European governments, passengers were distributed among Great Britain, France, Belgium and the Netherlands. USHMM documents the ship's arrival at Antwerp on 17 June.

Why the Outcome Matters

The passengers were not returned directly to Nazi Germany, but the story did not end safely for many of them. Germany invaded Belgium, the Netherlands and France the following year, placing many refugees once again under Nazi control. That later context is essential to understanding why the voyage became emblematic of failed refugee policy rather than simply an unsuccessful immigration journey.

Ship Versus Event

St. Louis also had an ordinary commercial history before and after the famous voyage: transatlantic passages, cruises and wartime accommodation use. Those phases should not be erased, but the 1939 voyage deserves careful treatment because the documentary record concerns individual refugees as much as the liner itself.

Interpretive Notes

For the 1939 voyage, the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum should take precedence over popular retellings. Passenger totals, landing decisions and later refugee outcomes are historical subjects where small inaccuracies can substantially distort the event.
