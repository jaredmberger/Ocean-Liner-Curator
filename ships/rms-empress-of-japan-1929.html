RMS Empress of Japan (1929)

Canadian Pacific · entered service 1930 · Ship Guide

Overview

RMS Empress of Japan was Canadian Pacific's major new trans-Pacific liner of 1930, built by Fairfield Shipbuilding & Engineering Company at Govan as yard no. 634. She linked Vancouver with Yokohama, Kobe, Shanghai and Hong Kong as part of Canadian Pacific's larger sea-and-rail network across the Pacific and North America.

Her long career crossed three identities and two oceans: Empress of Japan in Pacific service, Empress of Scotland after the wartime renaming of 1942, and finally Hanseatic under German ownership from 1958.

Canadian Pacific's Pacific Flagship

The ship's original importance was geographical as much as technical. Canadian Pacific marketed a through route in which ocean passage to Vancouver connected directly with the company's transcontinental railway. Empress of Japan was therefore part of a transport system stretching from East Asia across Canada to Atlantic connections rather than an isolated prestige vessel.

Her 1930s service reinforced Vancouver's role as Canadian Pacific's Pacific gateway and gave the company a modern flagship capable of carrying passengers, mail and cargo across the Far East route.

War and the Name Change

After Japan entered the Second World War, the name Empress of Japan became politically untenable for a British-registered Canadian Pacific ship. She was renamed Empress of Scotland in 1942 and continued in wartime transport service.

The renaming is best understood as a wartime identity change rather than the start of a new ship. The same hull later returned to civilian work under Canadian Pacific before being sold to German interests.

Hanseatic and the 1966 Fire

Hamburg Atlantic Line acquired the ship in 1957 and rebuilt her extensively for service as Hanseatic. The modernization increased tonnage and reshaped the passenger spaces for a postwar transatlantic and cruise market.

In September 1966 a serious fire in New York harbor spread from the machinery area through the ship. She was towed back to Hamburg, but repair was judged uneconomic and the vessel was broken up later that year. The loss ended a 36-year career spanning Canadian, British wartime and German passenger service.
