SS Queen of Bermuda

Furness Bermuda Line · entered service 1933 · Ship Guide

Overview

SS Queen of Bermuda was Furness Bermuda Line's purpose-built flagship for the short but commercially important New York–Bermuda route. Built by Vickers-Armstrong at Barrow-in-Furness as yard no. 681, she combined high passenger capacity with turbo-electric propulsion and a distinctive three-funnel profile.

Her career bridged resort travel, naval service and postwar cruising. Unlike liners designed around multi-day ocean crossings between continents, Queen of Bermuda served a compact route where the ship itself formed a major part of the holiday experience.

A Resort Liner

The New York–Bermuda trade occupied a distinctive place in liner history. The voyage was short enough that speed records mattered less than frequency, comfort and the ability to function as part of a leisure trip. Queen of Bermuda therefore combined liner engineering with a resort-oriented passenger experience.

Her original three-funnel silhouette also carried marketing value well beyond its purely technical function, helping make the ship instantly recognizable in Furness Bermuda Line publicity.

War Service

The Admiralty requisitioned Queen of Bermuda in August 1939 and converted her to an armed merchant cruiser. Historical RFA records her as pennant F73 and traces her departure from New York to Belfast immediately before the outbreak of war. She later moved into troop-transport duties before eventually returning to civilian ownership.

That wartime phase materially changed the ship's career: a vessel built for short luxury voyages became part of Britain's long-distance naval and troop-transport system.

Postwar Return and Rebuilding

Queen of Bermuda returned to the Bermuda trade after the war and remained a familiar presence into the 1960s. A major 1961–62 rebuilding radically simplified her appearance, replacing the original three-funnel arrangement with a single funnel and modernizing the passenger spaces.

The rebuild illustrates how operators tried to extend the commercial lives of expensive prewar ships rather than replace them immediately. She continued until 1966, when she was withdrawn and sold for scrap.
