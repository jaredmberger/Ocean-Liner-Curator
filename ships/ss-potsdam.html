SS Potsdam (1935)

Norddeutscher Lloyd · Bremen–East Asia Service · Ship Guide

Overview

SS Potsdam was one of three fast mid-1930s German liners built for the Bremen–East Asia service alongside Scharnhorst and Gneisenau. Launched by Blohm & Voss at Hamburg in January 1935, she had originally been ordered for HAPAG but was transferred to Norddeutscher Lloyd before entering service.

Her career is unusually long for an interwar German liner. After prewar Far East service, wartime accommodation and troop duties, and participation in the Baltic evacuation of 1945, she was taken over by Britain as a war prize. Rebuilt as the troopship Empire Fowey, she later passed to Pakistani ownership as Safina-E-Hujjaj and survived until 1976.

The 1935 East Asia Trio

Potsdam, Scharnhorst, and Gneisenau were conceived as a modern high-speed trio for the long German service to East Asia. Their common purpose masked significant engineering differences. The three ships were deliberately used to compare propulsion arrangements and hull forms under real operating conditions.

Potsdam was the Blohm & Voss-built member of the group and combined high-pressure Benson boilers with turbo-electric transmission. Scharnhorst also used turbo-electric drive, while Gneisenau used geared turbines. The ships therefore became both commercial liners and engineering test beds.

Design and Machinery

At roughly 17,500 gross tons, Potsdam was slightly smaller than her two running mates but comparable in overall role and performance. She had a modern bulbous bow, a single large funnel, and a long, low superstructure suited to warm-weather service through the Mediterranean, Suez, and the Indian Ocean.

Her machinery was particularly notable. Four Benson high-pressure boilers supplied steam to turbines driving electrical generators, which in turn powered electric propulsion motors connected to the twin screws. The arrangement reflected German interest in high-pressure steam and alternative transmission systems during the 1930s.

Far East Service

Potsdam began her maiden voyage on 5 July 1935. She joined Norddeutscher Lloyd's fast East Asia service, reducing journey times between Europe and major Asian ports at a moment when German shipping lines were attempting to rebuild their international standing.

The route linked Bremen and Bremerhaven with Mediterranean ports, the Suez Canal, South and Southeast Asia, China, and Japan. Her service context was therefore fundamentally different from the North Atlantic express trade dominated by ships such as Bremen and Europa.

Second World War Service

When war began in 1939, Potsdam returned to Germany and left commercial passenger service. The Kriegsmarine used her as an accommodation ship and later for troop movements.

A proposal to convert the ship into an auxiliary aircraft carrier under the code-name Project Elbe progressed far enough for preliminary work to begin in 1942, but the project was abandoned in 1943. She instead continued transport and accommodation duties.

Baltic Evacuation in 1945

During the final months of the war, Potsdam participated in the evacuation of German troops and civilians from the eastern Baltic. German maritime records credit her with multiple evacuation voyages and the transport of tens of thousands of people westward.

This late-war phase is a major part of the ship's historical record and should be treated separately from her prewar passenger service. The operational circumstances, passenger composition, and documentary sources are entirely different.

Empire Jewel and Empire Fowey

British forces seized Potsdam at Flensburg in May 1945. She passed to the Ministry of War Transport and was first renamed Empire Jewel, then Empire Fowey.

British authorities converted her for troop service, but her original high-pressure machinery proved difficult to operate reliably. A major rebuild replaced the boilers and propulsion plant and altered her accommodation. Under P&O management, Empire Fowey then spent years transporting British service personnel and families.

Safina-E-Hujjaj

In 1960 the ship was sold to the Pan-Islamic Steamship Company of Karachi and renamed Safina-E-Hujjaj. She entered a final phase carrying passengers and pilgrims under Pakistani ownership.

That service extended the life of a vessel designed in the early 1930s into the mid-1970s. She was finally broken up at Gadani in 1976, more than forty years after launch.

Why Potsdam Matters

Potsdam closes an important gap in the archive's coverage of Norddeutscher Lloyd's 1935 East Asia trio. Together with Scharnhorst and Gneisenau, she documents a short-lived but technically ambitious attempt to modernize Germany's Far East passenger service immediately before the Second World War.

Her unusually long postwar career also makes her a useful case study in adaptive reuse. The same hull served successively as German ocean liner, wartime auxiliary, British troopship, and Pakistani passenger vessel.

Interpretive Notes

Do not confuse her with the 1899 Holland America liner Potsdam: this guide covers the 1935 German ship associated with Scharnhorst and Gneisenau.

Ownership at completion is easy to oversimplify: HAPAG ordered the ship, but Norddeutscher Lloyd acquired her before she entered commercial service.

Her machinery changed substantially after the war: technical specifications from the 1935 liner should not be carried forward unchanged into the Empire Fowey period.
