SS Kronprinzessin Cecilie

Norddeutscher Lloyd · entered service 1907 · Ship Guide

Overview

SS Kronprinzessin Cecilie was the fourth and last of North German Lloyd's famous four-funnel express liners. Built by Vulkan at Stettin and entering service in 1907, she represented the mature end of the German express-liner program just as the new British turbine giants Lusitania and Mauretania were changing the competitive standard.

Her career divides cleanly into three documentary phases: prewar NDL passenger service, internment in the neutral United States after the dramatic August 1914 diversion to Bar Harbor, and U.S. military service as USS Mount Vernon. Treating those phases separately makes the ship's history clearer and prevents later military material from being mistaken for prewar passenger evidence.

The Last German Four-Funnel Express Liner

The German Maritime Museum's historical study places Kronprinzessin Cecilie at the end of the four-funnel express-liner sequence and notes an important design shift: her interiors moved away from the heavily ornamented historicism associated with earlier ships and received international notice for a more restrained approach.

That makes her more than the last member of a visual type. She marks a transition in the presentation of luxury at sea at the same moment the technical prestige race was moving toward turbine-powered British ships.

1914: The Bar Harbor Diversion

At the outbreak of the First World War, the ship was westbound from the United States carrying passengers and valuable cargo. Rather than continue into waters where British capture was likely, she returned to the United States and reached Bar Harbor, Maine. Naval History and Heritage Command records the episode as the beginning of an internment that lasted until American entry into the war.

The episode has generated a large folklore layer involving disguise, bullion, secrecy, and narrow escapes. Some elements are well documented; others have been embellished in later retellings. For collection work, a dated passenger list, newspaper, photograph, or official record should carry more weight than a generalized "treasure ship" story.

USS Mount Vernon

The United States commandeered the ship in 1917, renamed her USS Mount Vernon, and converted her for troop transport. NHHC records a full-load displacement of 29,650 tons, a speed of 24 knots, and a complement of 1,030 in wartime configuration.

On 5 September 1918, Mount Vernon was torpedoed by a German submarine while returning to the United States. The hit caused casualties and major damage, but the ship survived and reached port. This event deserves to be treated as a documented operational episode rather than compressed into the vague phrase "torpedoed but survived."

After the War

After repair and further transport work, the former liner passed through U.S. Army and Shipping Board control. She did not resume her prewar German passenger career. Long lay-up followed, and the ship was eventually broken up in 1940.
