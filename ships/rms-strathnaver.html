RMS Strathnaver

P&O · entered service 1931 · Ship Guide

Overview

RMS Strathnaver was the first of P&O's five interwar “White Sisters,” built by Vickers-Armstrong at Barrow-in-Furness as yard no. 663. Her white hull, buff funnels and turbo-electric machinery announced a deliberate break with the company's older visual and technical conventions.

P&O Heritage describes Strathnaver and sister Strathaird as ships that brought tangible improvements in passenger comfort: forced ventilation, running water in all cabins and a white hull believed to reduce summer heat. The first and third of her original three funnels were dummies, retained partly because a single working funnel was thought likely to look insufficiently powerful to contemporary passengers.

The White Sister Idea

The visual change was more than paint. P&O Heritage notes that the white hull was thought to lower summer temperatures by about four degrees Fahrenheit, while the new ventilation and plumbing standards improved daily life on the long voyage to India and Australia. These were practical responses to route climate as well as matters of style.

Strathnaver also carried cruising into her early program, showing that P&O was already using large mail liners flexibly rather than treating them as single-purpose route machines.

War Service

Requisitioned in January 1940, Strathnaver sailed in the first Australia and New Zealand convoy to the Near East. P&O's own fact sheet records North African and Mediterranean service, including landing troops at Algiers in November 1942 and operating under air attack while assisting survivors from other ships.

Across the war she steamed about 350,000 miles and carried 128,792 personnel according to P&O Heritage. Those figures make clear that wartime transport was not a brief interruption but a major second career.

Postwar Rebuild and Final Years

After the war Strathnaver underwent a substantial Harland & Wolff refit. When she re-entered commercial service in January 1950 she had only one funnel, more deck space and a revised passenger layout. In 1954 she was converted to one-class tourist accommodation for 1,252 passengers.

Her postwar changes reflect the same pressure seen across the liner industry: ships designed around prewar class structures were adapted for a larger, more price-sensitive travel market. She remained in service until 1962 before being sold for demolition in Hong Kong.
