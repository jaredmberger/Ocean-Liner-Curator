MS Gripsholm (1925)

Swedish American Line · entered service 1925 · Ship Guide

Overview

MS Gripsholm marked a genuine propulsion transition on the North Atlantic. Ordered as Swedish American Line's first newbuilding, she was constructed by Armstrong, Whitworth & Co. at High Walker on the Tyne and entered service in November 1925. Maritime museum and shipyard records identify her as the first motor passenger liner to operate regularly in transatlantic service, making her significance more specific than simply being an “early diesel liner.”

Her career then broadened beyond machinery history. She became an important Swedish American Line liner and cruise ship, served under neutral Swedish identity on United States-chartered diplomatic exchange and repatriation voyages during the Second World War, returned to commercial service after rebuilding, and finished her career in German ownership as Berlin.

Diesel on the Atlantic

The distinction between Gripsholm and a conventional steam liner was operational as well as technological. Her two large diesel engines drove twin screws and reduced fuel consumption relative to comparable steam machinery. A surviving builder's model at The Mariners' Museum describes her as the first motor passenger liner in regular transatlantic service and records a trial output of roughly 16,300 horsepower and an average of 17¾ knots.

Swedish American Line later paired the idea with the larger Kungsholm. Together the motor ships helped the company present diesel propulsion, electric elevators, swimming pools, and modern interiors as parts of a broader image of efficiency and contemporary luxury rather than as engineering novelties in isolation.

Passenger Service and Cruising

Gripsholm entered the Gothenburg–New York trade in November 1925 and soon combined scheduled Atlantic crossings with winter cruising. That dual use matters because Swedish American Line was among the companies adapting passenger ships for leisure itineraries before cruising became the dominant post-liner business model.

A major postwar rebuilding in 1949–50 altered the ship substantially, including her bow and external appearance. For artifact attribution, material from the original black-hulled ship should therefore be distinguished from post-rebuild imagery and from the later German Berlin period.

Exchange and Repatriation Voyages

During the Second World War, neutral Sweden and the ship's Swedish registry allowed Gripsholm to serve in a role unavailable to belligerent passenger liners. Beginning in 1942, she operated under U.S. charter on diplomatic exchange and repatriation voyages, transporting civilians, diplomats, wounded personnel, and other exchangees between belligerent spheres.

These voyages are unusually well documented because neutrality markings, passenger lists, diplomatic records, and survivor accounts all intersect. They should not be collapsed into a generic “troopship” narrative: the ship's value lay precisely in her protected exchange function.

Berlin and Disposal

Swedish American Line sold the ship to German interests in 1954. She was renamed Berlin in 1955 and later operated by Norddeutscher Lloyd. She remained in service until 1966. Sources agree that she was dismantled in Italy in 1966–67, but surviving references differ over whether to identify La Spezia, Trieste, or a specific breaking date; this guide leaves that final detail open rather than forcing a false precision.
