RMS Homeric (1913)

White Star Line · entered service 1922 · launched 1913 · Ship Guide

Overview

RMS Homeric was a ship whose commercial identity was created by the First World War. She had been ordered by Norddeutscher Lloyd as Columbus, yard no. 891 at F. Schichau's Danzig yard, and was launched on 17 December 1913. War stopped completion in 1914. She eventually emerged from the postwar reparations settlement as a British-owned White Star liner, entering service in 1922.

That origin explains both her strengths and her limitations. Homeric was large, comfortable and unusually steady, but she had been designed before the war around reciprocating machinery and a service speed below that of White Star's Olympic and Majestic. White Star therefore spent much of her career adapting a prewar German design to a postwar express-liner system.

Columbus Before Homeric

Norddeutscher Lloyd ordered the ship in 1912 as one of a pair of large liners. The First World War froze the project while the hull was still incomplete. Postwar settlement changed the ship's national and corporate future: the unfinished Columbus was ceded to Britain and acquired for White Star, while the second ship of the intended pair remained German and eventually took the name Columbus itself.

Completion took place at Danzig under British oversight. Secondary sources disagree on whether to date White Star's purchase or transfer to 1920 or 1921, so this guide treats the acquisition as a 1920–21 process rather than pretending the paperwork resolves to one universally cited date.

White Star Service

Homeric arrived at Southampton in January 1922 and entered the Southampton–New York service in February. Together with Olympic and the former German Bismarck, completed as Majestic, she gave White Star a three-ship postwar service assembled from very different design generations.

Her 18-knot speed made scheduling difficult beside the faster ships. White Star responded with an extended 1923 overhaul that converted the boilers from coal to oil and improved operating efficiency as well as speed. The change sharply reduced the engine-room labor required and brought service speed to roughly 19½ knots, but it could not turn the ship into a true high-speed express liner.

From Atlantic Liner to Cruiser

As traffic patterns shifted in the early 1930s, Homeric increasingly found work in cruising. Her Atlantic liner career effectively ended before the ship herself disappeared from service; she continued on cruises and special voyages into the Cunard-White Star period. This makes her a useful example of an older liner being redeployed as the economics of scheduled passage changed.

She was withdrawn in 1935 and sent for dismantling at Inverkeithing in 1936. Because the ship had been launched more than two decades earlier under a different name and nationality, surviving artifacts require unusually careful dating: a “Columbus” association is not automatically White Star, and a “Homeric” association cannot predate the postwar transfer.
