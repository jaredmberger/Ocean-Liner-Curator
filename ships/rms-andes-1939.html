RMS Andes

Royal Mail Lines · completed 1939 · Ship Guide

Overview

RMS Andes was Royal Mail Lines' new flagship for the Southampton–Rio de Janeiro–Buenos Aires service, built by Harland & Wolff at Belfast as yard no. 1005. She was completed on 24 September 1939—three weeks after Britain entered the Second World War—and therefore went almost directly from shipyard completion into government troop service.

Like Pasteur, she is best understood as a liner whose intended commercial debut was deferred by history. Her actual South America passenger career began only after the war, and her final phase anticipated the industry's wider transition from scheduled line voyages toward cruising.

A Maiden Voyage That Wasn't a Maiden Liner Voyage

Sources commonly label 26 September 1939 as Andes' maiden voyage because that was her first voyage after completion. It was not, however, the ceremonial beginning of the South America passenger service for which she had been designed. War had already intervened, and the ship proceeded into Admiralty-controlled troop work.

That distinction is worth making explicit. A completion date and a “maiden voyage” date can be technically correct while still giving a misleading picture of how a liner actually entered commercial service.

War and Delayed Commercial Career

Andes served as a troopship from 1939 until 1947 and emerged from the war intact. After release and refitting, she finally entered the Royal Mail passenger trade in 1948, running from Southampton through Rio de Janeiro to Buenos Aires.

The ship's refrigerated and general cargo capacity mattered alongside passenger accommodation. On the South America route she was not simply a floating hotel; she was part of an integrated mail, passenger and cargo system connecting Britain with Brazil and the River Plate.

From Royal Mail Flagship to Cruise Ship

By the end of the 1950s the economics of long-distance passenger lines were changing quickly. Royal Mail withdrew Andes from regular line service and rebuilt her for cruising around 1959–60. She then spent roughly a decade in a role that would become increasingly typical for surviving liners.

Her final cruise ended in May 1971, after which she sailed to Ghent for demolition. The career arc—designed as a prestige liner, commissioned into war, restored to line service, then converted to cruising—is almost a compact history of the mid-century liner business.
