SS Macedonia

Anchor Line · renamed 1873 · Ship Guide

Overview

SS Macedonia was an Anchor Line transatlantic steamship whose identity begins under another name: she was originally SS Iowa, a mid-nineteenth-century screw steamer later purchased by the Glasgow-based Anchor Line and renamed Macedonia in 1873. She belongs to the earlier iron-hulled Atlantic era, before the large twin-screw liners of the 1890s and early twentieth century became the public image of North Atlantic travel.

Her career is useful precisely because it is not a glamour-liner story. Macedonia represents the practical, transitional world of Glasgow–New York steamship service: mixed passenger, cargo, livestock, emigrant, and mail movement, with ship identities sometimes complicated by purchase, rebuilding, renaming, and changing tonnage figures.

Identity note: this page concerns Anchor Line’s Macedonia, formerly SS Iowa, not the better-known 1904 P&O liner Macedonia or later vessels using the same name.

Design & Construction Context

Macedonia belonged to an earlier generation of Atlantic steamships: smaller than the later express liners, but vital to the regularization of steam travel between Britain and North America. Her iron hull, screw propulsion, cargo capacity, and passenger function place her in the practical commercial world that preceded the luxury-obsessed “liner age” of the late Victorian and Edwardian decades.

The ship’s technical identity is complicated by her change of name and later rebuilding. In collecting terms, that means an item marked Iowa may be part of the same physical vessel’s biography, while an item marked Macedonia belongs to the Anchor Line identity after the 1873 renaming. Those labels are related, but they are not interchangeable without explanation.

Anchor Line Service

Anchor Line purchased the former Iowa and placed her into its Glasgow-based transatlantic system. After refurbishment and later renaming, Macedonia served in the line’s North Atlantic work, a field in which passenger traffic, emigrant travel, cargo, livestock, and scheduled sailing announcements often overlap in surviving documentation.

Unlike famous prestige liners whose identities are reinforced by abundant publicity material, ships like Macedonia often survive in more practical records: sailing notices, passenger lists, fleet lists, wreck reports, insurance or inquiry references, and occasional postal or cargo documentation. This makes the ship especially useful for evidence-first research because the best sources are often functional rather than decorative.

Wreck at the Mull of Kintyre

Macedonia left New York on May 16, 1881, bound for Glasgow with general cargo, American produce, cattle, crew, and cattlemen aboard. On May 30, in dense fog near the North Channel and the Mull of Kintyre, she grounded north of the Mull of Kintyre lighthouse. Contemporary and later wreck summaries report that all aboard escaped safely.

Salvage efforts removed cattle, a valuable horse, silver plate, papers, and materials, but the ship’s hull was badly damaged by the rocks and tides. She was ultimately declared a total wreck. The formal inquiry later found fault with the ship’s navigation and suspended the master’s certificate for a limited period.

Collecting Profile

Material connected to Macedonia is likely to be scarcer than ephemera from later, better-publicized liners. Plausible categories include passenger-list material, sailing advertisements, Anchor Line timetables or handbills, wreck-related press notices, official records, and references embedded in broader Anchor Line fleet documentation.

Because the ship changed name, provenance language matters. “SS Iowa / later Macedonia” may be accurate for pre-1873 material. “SS Macedonia, ex-Iowa” is better for post-renaming context. A seller description that collapses all phases into “Macedonia” without dates should be treated as a starting point, not a final attribution.

Interpretive Notes

The most important caution is not to confuse Anchor Line’s Macedonia with P&O’s later Macedonia, which appears much more often in postcard and naval-service contexts. A date, operator, route, or tonnage clue should be required before attaching an object to the Anchor Line vessel.

A second caution is the ship’s dual identity. Evidence that belongs to Iowa is not “wrong” for the vessel, but it belongs to an earlier name phase. For a museum-style description, that distinction should be preserved because it tells the story of ownership, refit, and changing commercial use.
