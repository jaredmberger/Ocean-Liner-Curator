RMS Queen Elizabeth

Cunard-White Star Line / Cunard Line · launched 1938 · Ship Guide

Overview

RMS Queen Elizabeth was built by John Brown & Company at Clydebank as yard no. 552, a larger and refined running mate for Queen Mary. Launched in September 1938, she was still being completed when the Second World War overturned every assumption behind her intended North Atlantic passenger career.

Her first Atlantic crossing in March 1940 was therefore not a commercial maiden voyage but a secret wartime dash from the Clyde to New York. Only after six years of military service and final passenger fitting-out did she make her first scheduled civilian crossing in October 1946.

The Secret 1940 Departure

Scotland's People records that wartime conditions halted normal fitting-out and that the unfinished liner was sent secretly to New York in 1940. On arrival she lay with Queen Mary and the French Normandie—the famous one-time concentration of the world's three largest passenger liners in the same harbor.

She subsequently went to Singapore and Australia for troopship conversion. This means the frequently cited 1940 “maiden voyage” and the 1946 passenger maiden voyage describe two different historical events; the guide keeps them separate rather than forcing one label to do both jobs.

Troopship Before Ocean Liner

Queen Elizabeth spent the war carrying Allied personnel over immense distances. By 1945 she was heavily involved in transatlantic movement of American troops. Her speed allowed her to operate without the ordinary convoy pattern used by slower transports, though wartime routing and security remained critical.

Only after the war was she completed in the form passengers had originally been meant to experience. Her civilian identity was therefore, unusually, the second major career of a ship built expressly as a passenger liner.

Postwar Express Service

From 1946 Queen Elizabeth and Queen Mary formed Cunard's two-ship express system between Southampton and New York. The pair offered the regularity and scale needed for Cunard's postwar Atlantic dominance, but by the 1960s jet aviation had fundamentally weakened the economics of the service.

Queen Elizabeth left Cunard service in 1968 after hundreds of Atlantic crossings. Attempts to reuse the enormous liner as a hotel and tourist attraction were short-lived, and she was ultimately sold to C. Y. Tung for conversion into a floating university.

Seawise University and the 1972 Fire

Renamed Seawise University, the ship reached Hong Kong in 1971 and underwent an extensive conversion. On 9 January 1972, shortly before planned sea trials, fire spread through the vessel. Hong Kong archival material records the destruction as arson; the burned hull capsized under the weight of firefighting water.

Later speculation about who set the fires and why is not settled evidence. The defensible conclusion is narrower: multiple fires destroyed the nearly converted ship, and the cause was treated as deliberate, with the responsible person or persons unresolved. Parts of the wreck were removed, while other remains were eventually covered by harbor reclamation.
