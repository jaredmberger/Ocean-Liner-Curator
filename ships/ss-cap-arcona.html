SS Cap Arcona

Hamburg Süd · entered service 1927 · Ship Guide

Overview

SS Cap Arcona was Hamburg Süd's 1927 flagship for the Hamburg–South America route, built by Blohm & Voss at Hamburg. She represented the mature interwar form of the company's prestige South Atlantic service and became one of the best-known German passenger ships of the period.

Her final history is radically different from that civilian identity. Requisitioned during the Second World War, she served as an accommodation ship and later in Baltic evacuation work. In April 1945 thousands of concentration-camp prisoners evacuated from Neuengamme were forced aboard. On 3 May 1945 British aircraft attacked the ships in Lübeck Bay, and the resulting catastrophe killed thousands.

Hamburg Süd Flagship

Before the war, Cap Arcona was a passenger liner associated with the Germany–Brazil–Argentina trade. That peacetime identity should be preserved rather than retroactively defined only by the events of 1945.

War and Requisition

The Kriegsmarine requisitioned the ship in 1940. She spent years as an accommodation vessel at Gotenhafen and was also used as a stand-in for Titanic during production of the 1943 German film. Late in the war she participated in evacuations from the eastern Baltic.

Neuengamme Prisoners

As the Neuengamme concentration-camp system was evacuated in April 1945, more than 9,000 prisoners were transferred to ships in the Bay of Lübeck. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum records that more than 4,500 prisoners were aboard Cap Arcona.

3 May 1945

British aircraft attacked Cap Arcona and Thielbek on 3 May. Neuengamme Memorial records roughly 7,100 victims in the ship bombings, including about 6,600 prisoners; USHMM gives about 7,000 deaths across the two ships and notes only about 600 prisoner survivors. Differences in totals reflect source scope and later reconstruction, so this guide avoids false precision for Cap Arcona alone.

Interpretive Notes

The 1945 catastrophe is a Holocaust and concentration-camp evacuation history as much as a maritime one. Passenger-liner ephemera, wartime naval material, and prisoner-transport evidence should remain distinct provenance categories.
