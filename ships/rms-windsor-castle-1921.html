RMS Windsor Castle (1921)

Union-Castle Line · entered service 1922 · Ship Guide

Overview

RMS Windsor Castle was the sister ship of Arundel Castle and the second of Union-Castle's great four-funnel Cape Mail liners. Launched by John Brown & Co. at Clydebank on 9 March 1921 and completed in 1922, she carried the visual language of the prewar prestige liner into a postwar Britain–South Africa service built around reliability, mail schedules and long-distance passenger traffic.

Like her sister, she was substantially modernized in the late 1930s and emerged with two funnels and a more modern profile. Unlike Arundel Castle, however, she did not survive the Second World War: while carrying troops in convoy KMF 11, she was hit by an aerial torpedo from a German He 111 on 23 March 1943 and eventually sank in the Mediterranean.

Sister to Arundel Castle

The pair should be interpreted together. They were conceived before the First World War, delayed by wartime disruption, and completed into a world in which the Cape Mail route was being rebuilt for the 1920s. Their four-funnel appearance gave Union-Castle a striking flagship identity without turning the route into a North Atlantic speed contest.

Name reuse is a persistent attribution problem. This page concerns the ship launched in 1921 and delivered in 1922, not Union-Castle's later Windsor Castle that entered service in 1960.

Modernization

During the 1930s Windsor Castle was modernized along lines comparable to Arundel Castle. The four-funnel profile disappeared, the hull and bow treatment were altered, and the ship emerged visually aligned with the newer generation of Union-Castle mail liners.

For photographs and postcards, funnel count therefore provides a useful date bracket. As always, it should support rather than replace printed and documentary evidence.

1943: Aerial Attack and Loss

On 23 March 1943 Windsor Castle was sailing toward Algiers in convoy KMF 11 when a German Heinkel He 111 torpedo bomber attacked. Naval operational records place the hit at about 02:54 near 37°28′N, 1°10′E. Escorting warships removed the embarked troops while tugs were sent toward the damaged liner.

The distinction matters because later retellings sometimes turn almost any wartime liner loss into a submarine story. In this case the immediate fatal damage came from an aerial torpedo, not a U-boat. The ship was abandoned and sank later that day.

Interpretive Notes

Windsor Castle documents both the prestige and vulnerability of the Cape Mail fleet. Her interwar material belongs to the commercial South Africa route; her wartime photographs and records belong to a troop-transport system operating under very different conditions. Those phases should remain visibly distinct.
