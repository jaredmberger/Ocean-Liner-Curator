SS Grosser Kurfürst

Norddeutscher Lloyd · launched 1899 · Ship Guide

Overview

SS Grosser Kurfürst was a large Norddeutscher Lloyd passenger-and-cargo liner built by F. Schichau at Danzig and launched on 2 December 1899. She developed the same broad commercial idea represented by the earlier Barbarossa-type ships: large carrying capacity, substantial accommodation across classes, and enough range and versatility to work long-distance routes beyond the prestige express service.

Her career became remarkably varied. She made early voyages to Asian and Australian ports, entered regular Bremen–New York service in 1900, assisted during the Volturno disaster of 1913, was interned and then seized in the United States, served as USS Aeolus, and later became a South American liner and then the Los Angeles–Honolulu passenger ship City of Los Angeles.

Global Combination-Liner Service

Naval History and Heritage Command describes Grosser Kurfürst as a steel-hulled, twin-screw passenger-and-cargo steamship with unusually large carrying capacity and accommodation extending from first class to steerage. Her maiden voyage was to Asiatic and Australian ports before she entered regular Bremen–New York service in the spring of 1900.

That sequence matters because the ship was not simply an Atlantic liner temporarily sent elsewhere. Long-distance flexibility was built into her commercial identity. Passenger lists, mail schedules, shipping intelligence, and port records from Australia and Asia therefore belong to the core history of the vessel rather than to a marginal side route.

North Atlantic Migration

From 1900 until the summer of 1914, Grosser Kurfürst worked regular North Atlantic service, with some sailings associated with Mediterranean ports. Like other large NDL combination liners, she carried a wide social range of passengers and substantial numbers of migrants.

For voyage-level research, route and date should be preserved alongside the ship name. The German spelling Großer Kurfürst and common transliterations such as Grosser Kurfurst or Grosser Kurfuerst can all appear in catalogs and databases.

The Volturno Rescue

In October 1913 the emigrant ship Volturno caught fire in severe North Atlantic weather. Grosser Kurfürst was among the vessels that answered the distress call and took part in the multinational rescue response. The episode became one of the best-known prewar Atlantic rescue operations.

Exact survivor totals assigned to individual rescuing ships vary among later summaries. The secure point is her documented participation. Where a precise number is important, it should be tied to the contemporary report or institutional source being used rather than harmonized from conflicting secondary accounts.

Internment, Seizure and Repair

When the First World War began, Grosser Kurfürst took refuge in American waters and was interned. After U.S. entry into the war on 6 April 1917, she was seized along with other German and Austro-Hungarian merchant ships. NHHC records a deliberate program of machinery damage carried out by members of the German crew before they were removed.

The Navy commissioned the ship under her German name on 4 August 1917 while repairs and transport conversion were underway. General Order No. 320 of 1 September changed her name to USS Aeolus. This is another case where one hull generated distinct archival identities that should remain separated in cataloging.

USS Aeolus

As Aeolus, the ship served with the Cruiser and Transport Force. NHHC records eight wartime round voyages carrying 24,770 men to Europe. After the Armistice she made seven additional turnaround voyages, returning about 22,080 healthy veterans and 5,018 wounded or sick personnel.

She was decommissioned on 22 September 1919 and transferred to the United States Shipping Board. Her military service was therefore substantial rather than incidental, accounting for a large surviving body of naval photographs, transport records, and wartime documentation.

Aeolus and City of Los Angeles

A major postwar reconstruction converted the former troopship back into a civilian liner. NHHC describes an extensive refit costing nearly $3 million, including remodeled accommodation, improved refrigeration, and conversion from coal to oil fuel. As civilian SS Aeolus, she entered Munson Line service to South America.

In 1922 she passed to the Los Angeles Steamship Company and became SS City of Los Angeles. Reconditioned again and painted in a bright Pacific passenger-service livery, she began Los Angeles–Honolulu service in September 1922 and remained on that route until sold for scrapping in 1937.

Collecting & Interpretive Notes

Surviving material may appear under three principal identities: Grosser Kurfürst, Aeolus, and City of Los Angeles. Each name corresponds to a distinct operator, route system, and visual context. A Hawaiian cruise brochure from the 1920s documents the same hull as a 1905 NDL passenger list, but not the same institutional history.

The ship is especially useful as a lifecycle case study. Her continuous hull passed through German imperial mail service, North Atlantic migration, maritime rescue, U.S. troop transport, South American commerce, and Pacific tourism. Following those transitions reveals more than a single-company narrative can.
