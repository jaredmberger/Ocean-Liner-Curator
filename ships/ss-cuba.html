SS Cuba

Cunard Line · 1864 · Ship Guide

Overview

SS Cuba was a mid-19th-century Cunard Line transatlantic steamship, built at Glasgow by Tod & McGregor and launched in 1864. She belonged to the generation of iron screw steamers that helped carry Cunard beyond the early paddle-steamer era and into a more regular, industrialized Atlantic service pattern.

Compared with later Cunard icons such as Mauretania, Aquitania, or Queen Mary, Cuba is a quieter research subject: smaller, earlier, and less represented in popular memory. That makes her especially useful as a reminder that the ocean-liner story was not built only by record-breakers and floating palaces. It was also built by workhorse vessels, mail contracts, emigrant traffic, and the routine discipline of repeated Liverpool–Queenstown–New York crossings.

This page treats Cuba as Cunard’s 1864 steamship. Because “Cuba” appears as both a ship name and a geographic term in maritime records, searches should be date- and line-anchored whenever possible.

Note on terminology: the phrase “ocean liner” is being used here in the broad historical sense of a scheduled transoceanic passenger/mail vessel, not in the later luxury-liner sense associated with the early 20th century.

Design & Construction (Context)

Cuba came from the period when transatlantic steamship design was moving toward greater reliability, regularity, and route discipline. She was not a giant by later standards, but her size and service profile fit the mid-Victorian liner world: practical, mail-conscious, and closely tied to the mechanics of migration and Atlantic communication.

The ship’s builder, Tod & McGregor of Glasgow, places her firmly within the Clyde’s important role in 19th-century steamship construction. For interpretation, this matters because Cuba belongs to an earlier technological chapter than Cunard’s famous turbine express liners. Her story is about the consolidation of steam service, not the glamour race of the Edwardian or interwar Atlantic.

Service History (Summary)

After her 1864 launch and maiden voyage later that year, Cuba served the Liverpool–Queenstown–New York route for Cunard. Surviving voyage listings place her repeatedly in North Atlantic service, including documented arrivals in New York during the early 1870s. Her career was part of the repetitive, timetable-driven work that made the Atlantic crossing a dependable commercial system.

In 1876, Cuba left Cunard ownership and was sold to D. Brown. Her conversion into the sailing ship Earl of Beaconsfield is an important identity shift: after that point, records and artifacts should be evaluated under the later name and configuration rather than assumed to represent Cunard passenger service.

Research Note: The Name “Cuba”

“Cuba” is a challenging search term because it can refer to the country, a port destination, a route advertisement, or one of several vessels. For this ship, reliable identification should include at least one of the following anchors: Cunard Line, 1864, Tod & McGregor, Liverpool–Queenstown–New York, 2,668 gross tons, or the later name Earl of Beaconsfield.

In cataloguing, avoid using a bare “SS Cuba” label when the evidence only says “Cuba.” A safer description might be: “Cunard Line SS Cuba context, 1860s–1870s, subject to verification,” unless the object itself provides a dated sailing, line imprint, or other confirmatory mark.

Collecting Profile (What Tends to Survive)

Material connected to a vessel of this era may include passenger-list references, printed sailing notices, engravings, newspaper items, agent advertisements, and occasional manuscript or correspondence material. Period objects are less likely to resemble the branded souvenir culture of later luxury liners. Paper context, typography, and date are therefore unusually important.

Because Cuba predates much of the highly collectible decorative ocean-liner ephemera market, the strongest items will usually be documentary rather than ornamental: dated passenger records, newspaper notices, Cunard advertisements, or material that links a named passenger to a known voyage.

Interpretive Notes (What to Avoid)

The main risk with Cuba is over-modernizing her story. She should not be framed like a later “floating palace,” nor should every Cunard item from the 19th century be assumed to belong to her. Her significance lies in route service, steamship development, and the practical Atlantic network that later prestige liners inherited.

A second risk is name confusion. Items associated with Havana, Caribbean cruising, or later Cunard visits to Cuba are not evidence for SS Cuba. The vessel name, line, date, route, and format must work together before an attribution becomes persuasive.
