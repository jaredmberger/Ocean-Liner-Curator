SS Rochambeau

French Line · 1911 · Ship Guide

Overview

SS Rochambeau was a French Line transatlantic liner built at Saint-Nazaire for the Compagnie Générale Transatlantique. Launched in 1911, she belonged to the practical middle tier of French Atlantic service: not a record-breaking prestige liner, but a substantial emigrant and passenger ship linking France and New York before, during, and after the First World War.

This guide treats Rochambeau as a working French Line vessel: important for route history, immigration material, postcards, menus, passenger ephemera, and the evolution of early twentieth-century marine propulsion.

Design & Role

Rochambeau was not conceived as a floating palace on the scale of the later Île de France or Normandie. Her value was operational: carrying a mixed passenger trade across the North Atlantic, with a heavy emphasis on steerage and third-class capacity in the prewar migration era.

Technically, she is notable because her machinery combined reciprocating steam engines with steam turbines, an arrangement that marked the experimental and transitional character of liner propulsion in the years before turbine installations became more standardized on many express ships.

Service History (Summary)

After entering French Line service in 1911, Rochambeau operated on the North Atlantic during a period when passenger traffic, migration, and national-line identity all overlapped. Her usual association was the France–New York trade, though wartime conditions altered routes and operating patterns.

During the First World War era, Rochambeau continued to appear in Atlantic service under changed conditions, including Bordeaux–New York operation. After the war, she returned to regular French Line work and underwent later refits before being withdrawn and sold for scrapping in 1934.

Name & Historical Association

The ship was named for Jean-Baptiste Donatien de Vimeur, comte de Rochambeau, the French commander whose forces played a major role in the American Revolutionary War. For a French liner serving the New York route, the name carried a clear Franco-American resonance: not merely a personal tribute, but a symbolic fit for a ship crossing between France and the United States.

Collecting Notes (Evidence-First)

Rochambeau material is most likely to appear as postcards, passenger-list references, printed ephemera, menus, luggage labels, or immigration-related documentation. As with other mid-tier liners, attribution often depends on whether the object names the ship directly, names the French Line generally, or is merely associated through family history or dealer description.

Treat broad “French Line” branding carefully. A CGT logo or generic French Line marking is not enough to confirm ship-specific use aboard Rochambeau. Stronger evidence includes dated sailing material, ship-named stationery, menus, deck plans, photographs with identifiable context, or a documented chain of custody.
