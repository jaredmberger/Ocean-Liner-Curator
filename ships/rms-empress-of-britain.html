RMS Empress of Britain

Canadian Pacific · entered service 1931 · Ship Guide

Overview

RMS Empress of Britain was Canadian Pacific's largest interwar passenger liner and the flagship of its North Atlantic service. Built by John Brown & Company at Clydebank as yard no. 530, she was designed around a seasonal operating pattern: fast summer service between Britain and Canada, then long winter cruises when the St. Lawrence route was less practical.

That dual-purpose career made her different from the pure express liners centered on year-round Southampton–New York competition. Her size, public rooms and cruising program were intended to keep an expensive flagship commercially useful across changing seasons.

Seasonal Flagship

Empress of Britain worked Canadian Pacific's Atlantic passenger and mail service during the navigation season and then shifted into cruising. The National Library of New Zealand records her winter world-cruise role and her 1938 visit to New Zealand, a useful reminder that interwar liners increasingly had to serve both transportation and leisure markets.

The strategy allowed Canadian Pacific to market the ship as both a fast imperial connection and a self-contained resort. That flexibility foreshadowed the growing importance of cruising as traditional liner economics became less dependable.

War Service

After the outbreak of the Second World War, the ship was requisitioned for troop transport. In 1940 she carried troops from Australia and New Zealand toward Europe and later moved military personnel through the Suez and Cape routes. Her peacetime seasonal flexibility gave way to wartime long-distance transport.

The 1940 Loss

On 26 October 1940, while sailing unescorted west of Ireland, Empress of Britain was hit by bombs from a German Focke-Wulf Fw 200 Condor and caught fire. Salvage and towage efforts began, and the burning ship did not immediately sink.

Two days later, on 28 October, U-32 torpedoed the damaged liner and she sank. Uboat.net records 623 people aboard, with 45 dead and 578 survivors. At roughly 42,348 GRT she became the largest merchant ship sunk by a German U-boat during the war. Keeping the two-stage sequence matters: the aircraft attack disabled and burned the ship; the submarine attack finished the loss.
