SS Letitia

Anchor-Donaldson Line · 1924 · Ship Guide

Overview

SS Letitia was an Anchor-Donaldson Line ocean liner launched in 1924 and completed in 1925 for North Atlantic service between Britain and Canada. Built at Govan by the Fairfield Shipbuilding and Engineering Company, she belonged to the practical interwar Atlantic world: comfortable cabin and tourist accommodation, substantial third-class capacity, and regular seasonal service rather than record-breaking express speed.

Letitia is especially useful as a research subject because her career passes through several sharply different identities: Anchor-Donaldson passenger liner, Donaldson Atlantic Line vessel, wartime armed merchant cruiser, troopship, hospital ship, government-owned Empire Brent, and later emigrant ship Captain Cook. A good attribution therefore needs to establish not only the ship name, but the period, operator, route, and function represented by the object or document.

This page treats Letitia as the 1924 vessel. A separate earlier Donaldson Line Letitia was built in 1912 and lost during the First World War, so dates are essential when cataloguing material under this name.

Design & Construction (Context)

Letitia was built for the Canadian trade rather than the luxury express race. Her design emphasized dependable North Atlantic service, mixed passenger accommodation, and seasonal flexibility. The ship’s profile and accommodation belong to the interwar “workhorse liner” category: important to migration, family travel, mail, cargo, and imperial or Commonwealth movement, even if less famous than the largest prestige liners.

For collectors, this makes Letitia valuable precisely because she is documentary. Passenger lists, landing cards, schedules, baggage labels, photographs, and menus can illuminate ordinary Atlantic movement between Britain and Canada. The strongest objects are those that fix a voyage, class of travel, route, or named passenger rather than simply presenting a decorative ship image.

Service History (Summary)

Entering service in 1925, Letitia operated in Anchor-Donaldson service on the Britain–Canada route. Summer service is commonly associated with Quebec and Montreal, while winter operations shifted toward ice-season ports such as Halifax and St. John. This seasonal route pattern is important when interpreting dated material, because a winter Halifax document and a summer Montreal passenger list may both be authentic to the same ship.

In the mid-1930s the corporate setting changed as Anchor-Donaldson gave way to Donaldson Atlantic Line arrangements. Printed matter from this period may therefore carry different line imprints even when referring to the same ship. A date and imprint should be preserved exactly in cataloguing rather than normalized into a single simplified operator label.

With the outbreak of the Second World War, Letitia was requisitioned and converted for naval service as an armed merchant cruiser. She later served as a troopship and then as a hospital ship. Those wartime identities created documentation that should be separated from ordinary passenger-service material, especially where medical, troop, convoy, or Admiralty records are involved.

Wartime Identity Note

Wartime Letitia material can be compelling, but it must be labelled carefully. HMS Letitia, HMHS Letitia, troopship references, hospital-ship usage, and ordinary commercial Letitia service are connected phases, not interchangeable categories. A photograph or document may be authentic to the ship while still belonging to a different operational identity than a seller claims.

A useful test is to ask what the object itself proves: ship name, military designation, date, port, unit, passenger, patient, convoy, or line imprint. If those anchors are missing, use cautious wording such as “associated with the wartime Letitia” rather than “from HMS Letitia” or “from the hospital ship.”

Postwar and Emigrant Service

After the war, Letitia continued through further identity changes. As Empire Brent and later Captain Cook, she participated in postwar movement, including emigrant traffic. This later phase is historically important, but it should not be folded back into the 1920s Anchor-Donaldson passenger-liner story unless the object clearly belongs to that earlier period.

For collection records, the later names should be treated as cross-references. A Captain Cook item may be connected to the same hull as Letitia, but it is not automatically an Anchor-Donaldson artifact. The name in use at the time of the item matters.

Collecting Profile (What Tends to Survive)

Likely surviving material includes passenger lists, postcards, shipboard menus, sailing schedules, immigration and landing documents, agency material, wartime photographs, hospital-ship references, and postwar emigrant ephemera. Because Letitia had several names and functions, cataloguing should lead with the evidence visible on the item: imprint, date, route, ship name, and operational status.

Strong attributions usually include at least one fixed anchor: Anchor-Donaldson or Donaldson Atlantic imprint, Canada route evidence, a dated voyage, military designation, hospital-ship context, or one of the later names tied to a documented postwar use. Without those anchors, a restrained description is safer than a confident claim.

Interpretive Notes (What to Avoid)

The biggest risk is collapsing Letitia into a single name across four decades of use. The same hull moved through commercial, naval, medical, government, and emigrant roles. The ship’s significance comes partly from that continuity, but object attribution depends on keeping each phase distinct.

A second risk is confusing the 1924 Letitia with the 1912 Letitia. Both names appear in North Atlantic and wartime contexts, and both are associated with Donaldson-related history. Dates, builder, tonnage, route, and fate should be checked before any object is assigned to one vessel or the other.
