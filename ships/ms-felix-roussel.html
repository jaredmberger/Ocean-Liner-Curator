MS Félix Roussel (1931)

Messageries Maritimes · entered service 1931 · Ship Guide

Overview

MS Félix Roussel was a large French motor liner built at Saint-Nazaire for Messageries Maritimes' Marseille–Far East service. Launched in December 1929 and entering service in February 1931, she belonged to the same generation as Georges Philippar and Aramis, combining diesel propulsion with elaborate route-specific interiors intended to evoke French Indochina and Southeast Asia.

Unlike Georges Philippar, whose commercial career ended after one round voyage, Félix Roussel survived into a long and varied second life. She was lengthened and re-powered in 1935, served through the Second World War as a troop transport, took part in the evacuation of Singapore in 1942, returned to Far East passenger service after the war, and was eventually sold as Arosa Sun before ending her career as a floating hotel.

A Far East Flagship

Félix Roussel was built for Messageries Maritimes' long route from Marseille through the Suez Canal to the ports of South and Southeast Asia and onward toward East Asia. Her first departure for Japan came on 26 February 1931.

The ship belonged to a generation of French motor liners designed around long-range efficiency rather than North Atlantic speed competition. Her diesel machinery, passenger capacity, and extensive lower-cost accommodation reflected the mixed social and commercial demands of the Far East service.

Khmer-Inspired Interiors

Her interiors were among the most distinctive elements of the ship. Messageries Maritimes records describe the public rooms as decorated in a Khmer style intended to recall French Indochina and the destinations served by the route.

First-class spaces included luxury cabins, balcony cabins, dining, music, smoking, children's, recreation, pool, and deck-sport areas. The decorative program was not merely ornamental; it deliberately connected the passenger experience aboard ship with the geography and cultural imagery of the route.

The Citroën Yellow Expedition

In April 1932 Félix Roussel took aboard vehicles and personnel from André Citroën's Central Asia expedition, commonly known as the Croisière Jaune or Yellow Expedition, at Hong Kong. The episode links the liner to one of the most heavily publicized interwar automobile expeditions.

This kind of transport role illustrates how a Far East liner could intersect with scientific, promotional, colonial, and technological enterprises far beyond ordinary passenger movement.

The 1935 Reconstruction

In 1935 the ship underwent a major modernization. Her hull was lengthened from 171.25 to 182.25 metres, the bow was altered, gross tonnage increased, and the Sulzer diesels were uprated from roughly 14,700 to 16,000 horsepower.

Service speed increased from about 16 to 18.5 knots. The reconstruction also changed her appearance substantially, making pre-1935 and post-1935 photographs distinguishable by the bow profile and proportions.

War Service

Félix Roussel remained on the Far East service until March 1940. In July she was requisitioned by the British at Suez and subsequently operated with a French crew for Allied transport work.

She made repeated troop voyages between India and Egypt and was later adapted to carry as many as 3,000 troops. Her wartime identity became fundamentally different from the elaborately decorated civilian liner of the 1930s.

Evacuation of Singapore

In February 1942 Félix Roussel took part in the evacuation of Singapore as Japanese forces closed on the city. Messageries Maritimes records emphasize that the ship operated under fire during the evacuation and was formally cited for her service.

This is one of the most important episodes in the ship's career. It places a French commercial liner directly inside the collapse of British power in Southeast Asia and gives her wartime history a significance extending well beyond French maritime service.

Free French Service

From mid-1942 the ship was operated by Free French personnel. She continued troop transport work through the remainder of the war and eventually returned to Singapore after its liberation, three years after helping evacuate the city.

The symmetry is striking: the same vessel that had escaped Singapore under fire in 1942 returned after the Japanese surrender as part of the Allied reoccupation of the region.

Return to Civilian Service

Félix Roussel was returned to France in April 1946 and resumed the Far East route. After several years she underwent another major refit at Dunkirk.

The wartime troopship conversion had largely destroyed the passenger cabins, so the accommodation had to be rebuilt extensively. The public rooms required less reconstruction, and much of the Khmer-inspired decorative identity survived. New cabin plans were developed under decorator Jules Leleu.

The 1950 Appearance

When Félix Roussel returned to the Far East route in September 1950, she looked markedly different. Her two square funnels had been replaced by a single oval funnel, creating a much more modern postwar silhouette.

The result was a ship with several clearly distinguishable visual eras: original straight-bow, two-funnel form; the lengthened 1935 version; wartime troopship grey; and the single-funnel post-1950 configuration.

Arosa Sun

Messageries Maritimes sold the ship in 1955 to Arosa Line, which renamed her Arosa Sun for service between Bremerhaven and Quebec. The sale placed the former French Far East liner into an entirely different passenger market.

When Arosa Line failed in 1960, the ship passed to Dutch ownership and was used as a floating hotel near IJmuiden. A fire in April 1963 damaged the vessel, but she remained in existence for years afterward and was finally demolished at Bilbao in 1974.

Why Félix Roussel Matters

Félix Roussel provides an almost ideal counterpoint to Georges Philippar. The two ships emerged from the same French Far East modernization program, but one was lost almost immediately while the other survived through war, reconstruction, sale, and reuse.

Her history also shows how adaptable an ocean liner could be across radically different settings: colonial passenger service, military evacuation, troop transport, postwar reconstruction, North Atlantic emigrant service, and finally static hotel use.

Collecting Profile

Surviving material is rich and visually varied. Messageries Maritimes collections preserve photographs of the ship before and after the 1935 modernization, wartime images, postwar photographs, interiors, and late-career views as Arosa Sun.

For attribution, the ship's changing silhouette is especially useful. Bow form, funnel arrangement, hull paint, and passenger-space configuration can help narrow a photograph or object to a specific period.

Interpretive Notes

Georges Philippar was a close sister, not an identical twin: the ships shared the same program and general design family but had separate construction and service histories.

The 1935 and 1950 rebuilds matter: dimensions, speed, external appearance, and passenger accommodation changed substantially.

Wartime passenger numbers should not be generalized: the ship's troop capacity after conversion does not represent her civilian passenger capacity.
