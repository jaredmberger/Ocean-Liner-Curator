SS Bremen

Norddeutscher Lloyd · entered service 1929 · Ship Guide

Overview

SS Bremen was Norddeutscher Lloyd's statement that Germany had returned to the front rank of North Atlantic passenger shipping after the First World War. Built at A.G. Weser in Bremen as yard no. 872, she combined a low, streamlined profile, four-shaft turbine machinery, a bulbous bow, very high service speed, and modern interiors into a ship intended to make weekly express service possible with only two vessels.

Her maiden voyage in July 1929 immediately won the westbound Blue Riband. Yet speed was only part of the design story: Bremen also carried a catapult-launched mail aircraft, turning the liner itself into a mobile aviation platform for the final leg of transatlantic mail delivery.

Blue Riband on the Maiden Voyage

Bremen sailed from Bremerhaven on 16 July 1929 under Commodore Leopold Ziegenbein and reached the Ambrose lightship after 4 days, 17 hours and 42 minutes, averaging 27.83 knots and taking the westbound Blue Riband from Mauretania. The homeward crossing also established a new record. The German Maritime Museum treats those passages as central to the ship's contemporary meaning: technical modernity, national prestige, and commercial marketing were inseparable.

The ship later raised the record again in 1933. Her high speed was not merely a publicity flourish; together with sister Europa, it supported the operational goal of a regular weekly service using two express liners rather than three.

Catapult Mail

The 1929 maiden voyage also demonstrated a different kind of speed. On 22 July, a Heinkel He 12 floatplane was catapulted from Bremen while the ship was still at sea and carried nearly 11,000 pieces of mail ahead to New York. ICAO's postal-history account says the system reduced delivery time by roughly 36 hours.

The arrangement was refined over subsequent voyages, and Europa received a comparable catapult installation in 1930. These systems belong in the liner story because they show ships, aviation, and postal infrastructure briefly working as a single transport network before long-range aircraft made the hybrid system obsolete.

War and Loss

At the outbreak of the Second World War, Bremen was away from Germany. Her final return voyage took her through Murmansk before she reached Bremerhaven in December 1939. She did not return to commercial passenger service and was used as an accommodation ship.

On 16 March 1941 she burned out at Bremerhaven in an arson fire. This guide no longer describes a neat “final disposal in 1946”: the German Maritime Museum records that the wreck was later moved into the Weser opposite Blexen and notes that remnants of the double bottom remained there. That is a messier ending, but it is the better-supported one.
