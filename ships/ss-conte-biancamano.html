SS Conte Biancamano

Lloyd Sabaudo · entered service 1925 · Ship Guide

Overview

SS Conte Biancamano was the third major “Count” liner built for Lloyd Sabaudo and an enlarged development of Conte Rosso and Conte Verde. Built by William Beardmore & Co. at Dalmuir as yard no. 640, she entered Genoa–New York service in November 1925 and was the last Italian passenger liner of her generation ordered from a foreign shipyard.

Her later career was exceptionally broad: Italian Line and Lloyd Triestino service, wartime seizure by the United States and conversion into USS Hermitage, massive global troop transport, postwar reconstruction, renewed Italian passenger service, and—uniquely among many major Italian liners—partial preservation in Milan.

The Third “Count”

Italian Liners Historical Society describes Conte Biancamano as a larger and improved version of the Beardmore-built Conte Rosso and Conte Verde. Her interiors were designed within the elaborate Coppedè tradition used across Lloyd Sabaudo's “Conti” fleet.

She later moved through the 1932 Italian shipping consolidation and into Lloyd Triestino service. Company name and route therefore matter when dating otherwise similar printed material.

USS Hermitage

When Italy entered the war against the United States, Conte Biancamano was interned in the Canal Zone. The United States seized the vessel, converted her at Philadelphia, and commissioned her as USS Hermitage on 14 August 1942.

Naval History records an extraordinary transport career spanning Operation Torch, the Pacific, India, troop and refugee movements, Normandy-era Atlantic service and Operation Magic Carpet. During U.S. Navy service the former liner steamed roughly 230,000 miles and carried nearly 130,000 passengers of many nationalities and statuses.

Return and Preservation

Returned after the war, the ship was extensively reconstructed before resuming Italian passenger service. The postwar Conte Biancamano was therefore not visually identical to the 1925 vessel; photographs and deck plans should be dated accordingly.

When the ship was finally dismantled, significant portions of her superstructure and interiors were saved and reassembled at Milan's national science and technology museum. That survival gives Conte Biancamano an unusual material afterlife among demolished ocean liners.

Interpretive Notes

Conte Biancamano, USS Hermitage, and the reconstructed postwar liner are best treated as distinct documentary phases. The museum remains add another layer: surviving ship fabric can be studied directly rather than only through photographs and printed ephemera.
