RMS Mauretania

Cunard Line · 1907 · Ship Guide

Overview

RMS Mauretania was a British transatlantic express liner built for the Cunard Line and entered service in 1907. Together with her sister RMS Lusitania, she represented a decisive shift toward turbine-powered speed and modern long-distance reliability on the North Atlantic—an “express liner” concept that shaped passenger expectations well into the interwar era.

This page is written as a reference doorway: it summarizes widely documented facts, flags uncertainty where it exists, and avoids turning repeated anecdotes into “record” without evidence.

Commercial Ambition and National Strategy

Cunard’s new express liners were a response to international competition, particularly German advances in Atlantic passenger service. British government support linked commercial competitiveness to the availability of vessels for national service. A retrospective discussion in the United States Congressional Record of 1923 describes a £2.6 million construction loan and £150,000 annual payment for the two-ship arrangement. These figures concern Lusitania and Mauretania together, not each ship individually. The original British agreement remains to be checked for exact terms and payment categories.

Design & Construction (Context)

Mauretania belongs to a pivotal moment when turbine propulsion moved from novelty to strategic advantage. The ship’s identity is therefore tightly bound to speed: public marketing, press attention, and passenger expectations all framed her as a modern “express” instrument rather than simply a large floating hotel.

For collectors, this “speed-era Cunard” context is useful: ship stationery, menus, printed sailing lists, postcards, and souvenirs often participate in that prestige narrative. Attribution improves when you can match typography, printer marks, and branding language to a narrow time window (prewar vs wartime vs postwar).

Engineering evidence

The Newcastle University Marine Technology Special Collection identifies a machinery agreement dated 14 July 1905, construction photographs and contemporary technical publications from 1907. Its account of cost-plus contracting provides context for the uncertainty involved in building machinery on this scale. The collection guide is a finding aid; the underlying contract and technical publications require direct examination.

Service History (Summary)

From 1907 onward, Mauretania operated primarily on the North Atlantic and developed a reputation for fast, consistent passages. In 1909 she is widely credited with capturing the Blue Riband and became one of the signature ships of the pre–First World War era.

At the outbreak of war, her proposed armed merchant cruiser employment was reconsidered because of her size and fuel requirements, according to Northumberland Archives. Her subsequent troop transport and hospital-ship assignments should be distinguished from that initial intention. After the war, she returned to commercial service in a changed travel market, now competing with newer ships and evolving passenger expectations.

In the 1920s and early 1930s, Mauretania continued service with modifications over time, but the economics of operation increasingly favored newer, more efficient liners. She was withdrawn in 1934 and sent for dismantling in 1935 (see Tyne Built Ships service chronology).

Later History & Final Disposition

Mauretania’s scrapping created an “afterlife” that matters directly to collectors: fittings and decorative elements could be dispersed, repurposed, or sold, and claims of origin can be tempting to accept at face value.

Ocean Liner Curator treats “from Mauretania” claims the way it treats all high-value provenance assertions: look for credible documentation, detailed match points to period photographs, and a traceable chain of custody. When those are absent, responsible labels include “attributed,” “reported,” or “unverified.”

Speed, Scale, and the Turbine Generation

Royal Museums Greenwich describes Mauretania as both the world's largest liner when she entered service in 1907 and, within weeks, the fastest after surpassing her sister Lusitania. Her four Parsons turbines driving four screws placed her at the center of the transition from reciprocating machinery to turbine-powered express liners.

Her long association with the Atlantic speed record matters because it was sustained rather than momentary. Mauretania remained the benchmark until Bremen took the Blue Riband in 1929.

Two Wartime Roles

During the First World War Mauretania served in both troopship and hospital-ship roles. Royal Museums Greenwich records that she carried about 76,000 troops during wartime service.

These configurations should be separated from civilian Cunard service when interpreting photographs and plans. The same hull could be painted, partitioned, staffed, and used very differently depending on the assignment.

Oil Conversion and the Final Express Era

Withdrawn in 1921 for conversion from coal to oil firing, Mauretania returned to service the following year and continued alongside Berengaria and Aquitania. Her survival into the 1930s made her a living bridge between the prewar turbine revolution and the next generation of giant express liners. The 1921–1922 chronology follows Tyne Built Ships; the Smithsonian model catalog gives a differing conversion chronology, which remains to be reconciled with yard records.

Selected service chronology

  1. 1907–1914: commercial express service.
  2. 1914: initial wartime requisition and reconsideration of intended role.
  3. 1915–1919: wartime transport and hospital assignments, followed by return to civilian service.
  4. 1921–1922: oil-firing conversion and refit, subject to the source discrepancy noted above.
  5. 1934: withdrawal from commercial service.
  6. 1935: final voyage to Rosyth and dismantling.

Interpretive Notes

Most popular narratives around Mauretania revolve around “records” and “the fastest”—and those claims can be true in broad shape while still being vulnerable to oversimplification. Speed records depend on definitions (eastbound vs westbound, distance measured, timing method, weather, routing), and secondary sources sometimes compress a complex record history into a single clean phrase.

Treat the ship as a documented entity with multiple “versions” over time: prewar express liner, wartime service ship, postwar commercial veteran. For collecting, that approach helps avoid category errors (e.g., presenting postwar printed material as if it were prewar, or claiming a generic Cunard souvenir is ship-specific without evidence).

Final Departure from Southampton

On 1 July 1935, Mauretania left Southampton for the last time, bound for the shipbreakers at Rosyth, Scotland. A Southampton dispatch dated 2 July, printed in The Penguin on 3 July 1935, reported her departure the previous day amid playing bands, cheering crowds, and decorated small craft following in her wake. This was her final voyage to the breakers, not a final commercial passenger crossing.
