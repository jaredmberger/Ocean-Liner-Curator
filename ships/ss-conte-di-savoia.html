SS Conte di Savoia

Italia Flotte Riunite / Italian Line · entered service 1932 · Ship Guide

Overview

SS Conte di Savoia was conceived separately from Rex but entered service beside her after the consolidation of the major Italian shipping companies into Italia Flotte Riunite. Built at Cantiere San Marco in Trieste as yard no. 783, she represented a distinctly more modern design vocabulary and became famous for an engineering experiment aimed at one of ocean travel's oldest discomforts: rolling.

Three large Sperry gyroscopes were installed as an anti-roll system. Contemporary promotion treated the equipment as proof of technical modernity, and regional archival material identifies Conte di Savoia as the first transatlantic liner to mount such gyroscopic stabilizers.

The Anti-Roll Experiment

The gyroscopes are the ship's most distinctive technical feature, but they deserve careful wording. They did not eliminate motion at sea; they were intended to reduce roll, and doing so consumed power. Regional Ligurian archival material explicitly describes the system as reducing rolling at the expense of engine power.

That distinction matters because contemporary advertising naturally exaggerated comfort claims. The historical value lies not in saying the ship “did not roll,” but in showing how seriously liner companies were beginning to engineer passenger comfort as a measurable mechanical problem.

Service and Speed

Conte di Savoia sailed from Genoa on her maiden voyage on 30 November 1932. Near the American coast she developed a leak in the machinery space and arrived several hours late, another reminder that technically ambitious ships could have difficult debuts. In March 1933 she averaged about 27.53 knots from Gibraltar to New York, a strong performance but not enough to take the Blue Riband from Europa.

Her interiors and machinery made her a complement rather than a duplicate of Rex. The two ships shared the same prestige route but had different shipyard origins, design approaches and public identities.

War, Fire and Final Disposal

With Italy approaching entry into the Second World War, Conte di Savoia made her final Atlantic crossing in 1940 and was laid up in the Venetian lagoon near Malamocco. On 11 September 1943, amid the confusion following the Italian Armistice, German aircraft attacked the ship and she burned for roughly two days before being settled to prevent capsizing.

The hulk was raised after the war and reconstruction was studied but never carried out. She was sold for demolition in 1950, became the focus of a labor dispute over where the breaking work would be assigned, and finally reached Monfalcone on 24 April 1950. Dismantling was completed in 1951.
