SS Roma

Navigazione Generale Italiana · entered service 1926 · Ship Guide

Overview

SS Roma was one of Navigazione Generale Italiana's largest liners of the 1920s, built by Ansaldo at Sestri Ponente and entering service in September 1926. Together with the motor liner Augustus, she represented NGI's effort to replace the delayed Duilio/Giulio Cesare generation at the top of its North Atlantic service.

Her later history is extraordinary because the passenger liner was almost completely transformed during the Second World War. Taken over for conversion into the aircraft carrier Aquila, her interior, machinery, hull form and superstructure were radically altered. The conversion was never completed before Italy's 1943 armistice.

NGI's 1920s Flagship Generation

Roma and Augustus were not engineering twins in every respect, but they formed a clear commercial pair. Their arrival displaced Duilio and Giulio Cesare from primary North Atlantic work and helped reshape NGI's route assignments in the late 1920s.

The ship also illustrates the Italian industry's willingness to combine traditional luxury with national decorative programs. Surviving interior material should be kept distinct from later Aquila documentation, which represents a near-total change in function and form.

From Roma to Aquila

After Italy entered the Second World War, Roma was laid up and selected for carrier conversion. Work accelerated in 1941. The ship was gutted internally, lengthened, given deep side bulges and a hangar/flight-deck system, and fitted with entirely new machinery capable of much higher speed.

This was not a simple naval adaptation of a liner. By the time the conversion approached completion, the original passenger ship had been structurally transformed. Cataloging “Roma/Aquila” material therefore requires a hard provenance break rather than treating the carrier as merely a wartime configuration.

Unfinished Warship

Aquila was still incomplete when Italy signed the 1943 armistice. German and Italian Social Republic forces took control of the vessel. She was later damaged by sabotage and postwar authorities ultimately judged completion uneconomical.

The hull was raised and broken up in the early 1950s. The famous carrier conversion can easily overshadow Roma's fifteen years as a passenger liner; this guide deliberately preserves both identities.

Interpretive Notes

Material should be labeled either SS Roma or aircraft carrier Aquila according to date and physical state. The two names describe successive identities of one hull, but the conversion was so extensive that collapsing them obscures more than it clarifies.
