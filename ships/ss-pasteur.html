SS Pasteur

Compagnie de Navigation Sud-Atlantique · completed 1939 · Ship Guide

Overview

SS Pasteur was built at Saint-Nazaire for Compagnie de Navigation Sud-Atlantique as a large French liner for the Bordeaux–South America trade. Launched in February 1938 and completed in 1939, she reached readiness at almost exactly the moment European war made the intended commercial program impossible.

That timing defines the ship better than any conventional maiden-voyage narrative. Pasteur became a troop transport under British wartime control, survived the conflict, and only much later received a sustained passenger-liner career after purchase and reconstruction by Norddeutscher Lloyd as Bremen.

A Liner Without a Normal Debut

Pasteur is sometimes summarized as though she simply entered South Atlantic passenger service in 1939. That compresses away the central fact: although completed for that trade, the outbreak of war prevented a normal peacetime career from taking shape. The ship was soon carrying military personnel rather than the passengers for whom her public rooms had been designed.

For attribution work this distinction matters. Material confidently dated to the late 1930s can belong to the planned French liner identity even when there was no long, settled prewar service history behind it.

War Service and Return to France

From 1940 Pasteur operated under British control and was used extensively as a troopship. Sources also associate her with medical and repatriation work during the wartime period. She survived a type of service that consumed many contemporary liners and returned to French ownership after the war.

Attempts to establish a durable postwar commercial role under her original name did not ultimately give her the career for which she had been built. She was laid up before sale to Norddeutscher Lloyd in 1957.

Rebuilt as Bremen

The German purchase transformed rather than merely renamed the ship. A major 1957–59 reconstruction produced the fifth Bremen, which entered North Atlantic service for Norddeutscher Lloyd in 1959. The Mariners' Museum catalog preserves the continuity explicitly, identifying its Bremen material with the earlier Pasteur hull.

Later ownership brought further names and uses, including cruise and accommodation roles. In 1980, while being towed toward Taiwanese breakers under her final identity, the old liner sank in the Indian Ocean. Her extraordinary lifespan therefore joins three different liner worlds: unrealized French prewar ambition, wartime transport, and postwar German Atlantic rebuilding.
