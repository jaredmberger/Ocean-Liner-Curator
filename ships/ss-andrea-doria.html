SS Andrea Doria

Italian Line · entered service 1953 · Ship Guide

Overview

SS Andrea Doria was one of the principal ships with which the Italian Line rebuilt its North Atlantic passenger service after the Second World War. Built by Ansaldo at Sestri Ponente as yard no. 918, she combined modern machinery, extensive public art and a three-class accommodation plan intended to present postwar Italy as technologically and culturally renewed.

Her historical significance extends well beyond the 1956 collision that ended her career. The ship belongs equally to the story of European reconstruction, design patronage and the last great phase of scheduled transatlantic liner service before jet aviation changed the market.

Postwar Design and National Identity

Fondazione Ansaldo's archive records the ship's launch, maiden voyage and the deliberate use of leading Italian designers and artists in her interiors. Gio Ponti and Nino Zoncada were among those involved in first-class spaces, while Gustavo Pulitzer Finali designed other rooms. The result was not simply decorative luxury; it was a state-of-the-art liner used to project an image of an inventive, modern Italy after wartime destruction.

That context matters because later memory often reduces Andrea Doria to a disaster story. For most of her short career she was a functioning prestige liner on the Genoa–New York service, paired in concept with Cristoforo Colombo.

The Collision

On the night of 25 July 1956, in heavy fog south of Nantucket, the Swedish American liner Stockholm struck Andrea Doria on her starboard side. The impact opened a large breach and the Italian ship developed a severe list. That list quickly made the port-side lifeboats unusable and complicated evacuation from the remaining boats.

The casualty total is commonly reported as 51 or 52 depending on whether accounting conventions include all deaths associated with both ships. The U.S. Coast Guard chronology records 52 deaths; other contemporary and later summaries cite 51. This guide therefore avoids presenting the difference as settled when sources count the loss differently.

Rescue and Sinking

The disaster is also one of the major rescue stories in modern passenger-ship history. Nearby vessels responded to the distress calls, including Île de France, Cape Ann, Stockholm herself and others. The U.S. Maritime Administration records Cape Ann alone rescuing 129 people in repeated lifeboat trips.

Andrea Doria remained afloat for about eleven hours after the collision before capsizing and sinking on the morning of 26 July. The long interval permitted the evacuation of the overwhelming majority aboard, even though the ship's list disabled much of her own normal lifeboat capacity.
