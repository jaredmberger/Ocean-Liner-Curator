SS Île de France

Compagnie Générale Transatlantique (French Line) · entered service 1927 · Ship Guide

Overview

SS Île de France was one of the ships that changed what “modern” could mean aboard a North Atlantic liner. Built at Saint-Nazaire for Compagnie Générale Transatlantique and entering service in June 1927, she was not designed to win a speed race. Her influence came instead from contemporary French decorative art, passenger experience, and the ability of a national liner to function as a showcase for design.

Her career later expanded far beyond interwar glamour. She carried troops and matériel during the Second World War, was rebuilt dramatically after 1947, and in July 1956 became the principal rescue ship at the loss of Andrea Doria, taking aboard 753 survivors—more than any other vessel at the scene.

Modern French Design at Sea

The ship's interiors followed the direction established by the 1925 Exposition internationale des arts décoratifs et industriels modernes. Designers and artists associated with contemporary French decorative arts were used throughout the passenger spaces. The result was important because it looked forward rather than imitating historic European palace styles that had dominated earlier first-class interiors.

That distinction matters when describing Île de France as an “Art Deco liner.” The label is useful, but the evidence is stronger when tied to specific spaces, designers and the post-1925 decorative movement rather than treated as a generic synonym for luxury.

Catapult Mail Before Bremen

Île de France also participated in one of the more unusual interwar efforts to accelerate transatlantic communication. A stern aircraft catapult was installed in 1928 so a seaplane carrying mail could leave the ship before arrival. The French experiment predated the better-known German catapult system later used aboard Bremen and Europa.

ICAO records that the French operation ended in 1930 as cost, structural demands, and improving aircraft range weakened the case for ship-launched mail planes. It is a useful reminder that liners were briefly part of an experimental air-sea network rather than isolated transport technologies.

War and Rebuilding

When war began in September 1939, Île de France reached New York and was initially laid up. She was subsequently used for Allied transport work and converted for heavy wartime service. The U.S. Maritime Administration records her operating under British control during the war before return to CGT in 1945.

Beginning in 1947, Penhoët rebuilt the ship over roughly two years. The work changed both capacity and appearance: the original three-funnel silhouette became two funnels, and passenger accommodations were reorganized for the postwar market. She returned to transatlantic service in 1949 as a visibly different ship.

Andrea Doria, 1956

On the night of 25–26 July 1956, Île de France was eastbound from New York when she received the distress call from the stricken Italian liner Andrea Doria. Captain Raoul de Beaudean turned the fully loaded French liner back toward the casualty.

The Maritime Administration's Gallant Ship Award record states that Île de France launched thirteen lifeboats and brought 753 survivors aboard. Her size, lighting, medical capacity and available boats made her arrival a turning point in the rescue. The episode is unusually well quantified and should be described from the official rescue record rather than from later dramatization.

Withdrawal

By the late 1950s, jet aviation was rapidly changing the North Atlantic passenger market. CGT sold the aging ship for scrap. She departed Le Havre for the last time in February 1959 and was dismantled in Japan after a final, highly theatrical use as a movie prop. Her long career therefore closed at almost exactly the moment when the transport system she had helped define was being displaced by the airliner.
