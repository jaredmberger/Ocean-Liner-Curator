Thomas Andrews Aboard Olympic

Passage I · A new liner at sea

Olympic's first Atlantic crossing

In June 1911, White Star Line's Olympic entered transatlantic service. Thomas Andrews of Harland & Wolff traveled aboard the new ship and recorded practical observations about how the vessel worked in service. The significance of these notes lies in their specificity: they show a designer assessing a ship after passengers and crew had begun using its spaces.

The notes belong to the history of Olympic in her own right. They also illuminate the design culture surrounding her near-sister Titanic, then approaching completion—but the two ships must not be treated as interchangeable.

Passage II · The physical record

A report in numbered observations

The surviving documentary record is not a complete bound notebook available for inspection in one place. A Christie's auction description identifies a carbon-copy manuscript report dated 14 June 1911, consisting of ten single-sided sheets fastened together. The catalog transcribes selected passages and establishes a valuable provenance lead, but an auction description is not a substitute for examining every manuscript leaf.

What the catalog establishes

Physical manuscript material existed and was described for sale; its numbered observations were not merely a story passed down in later liner histories. The extent, present custody, and relationship of all surviving sheets should be researched independently.

Christie's manuscript lot record

Maritime historian Mark Chirnside has published a reconstruction of the numbered notes, drawing on material he acquired and on other research. His transcription is a secondary presentation of primary material; it should be cited as such when the original leaf has not been examined.

Passage III · A shipbuilder's eye

Details that mattered aboard ship

Andrews' observations ranged across the liner's public rooms, working arrangements and technical performance. They were not a single dramatic verdict on Olympic, but a sequence of practical matters that could be evaluated and, where appropriate, corrected.

Passenger arrangements

Dining-room tables

The surviving account includes a recommendation to increase the number of small two-person tables in the first-class restaurant. Such a detail reveals attention to the lived experience of a new passenger interior.

Working safety

Mooring operations

Andrews noted the danger of third-class passengers approaching mooring ropes. This was a practical operational concern, not proof of a particular later accident.

Bridge arrangements

Weather protection

The surviving record relays Captain Edward Smith's suggestion concerning protective windows on the bridge. The observation should not be mistaken for proof that a particular modification was carried out.

Engineering

Vibration and motion

The notes discuss a vertical pulsation at certain engine speeds while distinguishing it from more general vibration. This illustrates the value of assessing machinery under operating conditions.

These examples are paraphrases from published descriptions and reconstruction, not purported facsimile transcriptions of manuscript pages.

Passage IV · The sister ship

What the notes can—and cannot—tell us about Titanic

Because Titanic was still being completed when Olympic entered service, it is reasonable to investigate whether experience from the maiden voyage informed later decisions. But the existence of a recommendation does not demonstrate that it was implemented on Olympic, transferred to Titanic, or motivated by any subsequent disaster.

Evidence threshold

To establish a design change, compare the dated observation with shipyard correspondence, plans, alteration records, photographs or other contemporary documentation. Similarities between the sister ships alone cannot prove causation.

Read the report first as evidence of how Olympic was assessed, rather than as a retrospective prediction of Titanic's fate.

Passage V · The incomplete record

What remains missing?

Chirnside's July 2025 reconstruction describes a sequence numbered 1–56, but the published compilation does not reproduce every entry. In that reconstruction, numbers 28–39 and 47–55 remain absent: 21 numbered observations in all. This is a statement about the published reconstruction at that date, not proof that those leaves no longer exist anywhere.

Passage VI · The documentary trail

How we know—and what we do not

The following sources serve different functions. The auction catalog describes manuscript material; the historical reconstruction brings dispersed observations together; the ship guide provides broader vessel context. None should silently stand in for another.

  1. Christie's, Thomas Andrews manuscript report concerning Olympic, dated 14 June 1911. Auction-catalog description of surviving physical manuscript sheets, with excerpts. Catalog record.
  2. Mark Chirnside, “Thomas Andrews' Olympic Maiden Voyage Notes,” updated 1 July 2025. Historian's reconstruction and discussion of the surviving numbered observations and missing entries. Research article.
  3. Ocean Liner Curator, RMS Olympic ship guide, for vessel chronology and contextual interpretation. This is an internal research guide, not a primary manuscript source.
Editorial method.

This exhibition uses no invented notebook facsimiles, no unattributed manuscript quotations, and no assumption that missing observations are permanently lost. Claims about changes to Titanic remain questions unless independently documented. Manuscript custody and completeness are subjects for continued research.

In the smallest adjustments of a great ship, the working mind of its designer becomes visible.
