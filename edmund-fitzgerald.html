Edmund Fitzgerald: The Last Voyage

On the evening of 10 November 1975, a 729-foot Great Lakes freighter vanished in a violent Lake Superior storm. All 29 people aboard were lost. The ship left behind a wreck, an official record, and questions that still resist a single certain answer.

November 9–10, 1975 · Lake Superior · In memory of 29 mariners

About this exhibition. The Edmund Fitzgerald was a Great Lakes bulk carrier, not a transatlantic passenger liner. Her story is included here as part of the wider history of ships, maritime labor, navigation, and disaster. The narrative distinguishes documented events, official conclusions, and unresolved interpretations. No speculative depiction of the sinking is presented as fact.

Passage I · The working freighter

A ship built for the iron trade

Built at Great Lakes Engineering Works in River Rouge, Michigan, and launched in 1958, Edmund Fitzgerald was among the largest vessels on the Great Lakes. At approximately 729 feet in length, she carried bulk iron-ore cargo through a system of inland ports, locks, and industrial waterways. Her work was essential to the movement of raw material to steelmaking centers.

Unlike the passenger vessels featured elsewhere in this archive, her accommodations and daily routines served a working crew. Her historical significance lies not in luxury but in the scale, skill, and risk of Great Lakes commercial shipping.

Passage II · November 9, 1975

Departure from Superior

On the afternoon of 9 November, Captain Ernest M. McSorley took Edmund Fitzgerald out of Superior, Wisconsin, bound for Detroit. The freighter Arthur M. Anderson, commanded by Captain Bernie Cooper, sailed on a similar course and remained in radio contact. With a major storm developing, the captains chose a northern route across Lake Superior, hoping to benefit from shelter along the Canadian shore.

A voyage in company

The presence of Arthur M. Anderson is central to reconstructing the final passage. Its crew supplied radio accounts, observations of weather and sea conditions, and evidence about the Fitzgerald's final reported difficulties.

Passage III · November 10, 1975

Lake Superior in a November gale

As the storm crossed the lake, the freighters faced violent winds, heavy seas, and deteriorating visibility. The National Weather Service reports ship observations of winds reaching 50 knots and waves approaching 20 feet. Conditions and measurements varied by location and time; retrospective wave estimates should not be mistaken for a direct measurement at the exact moment of sinking.

During the afternoon, Fitzgerald reported damage and operational difficulties, including a list and the loss of radar capability. The crew continued toward the relative shelter of Whitefish Bay.

Meteorological record

Reconstructing the storm

Weather observations, forecast records, and later analysis help establish the danger confronting the ships, but they cannot alone demonstrate the precise physical sequence that destroyed Fitzgerald.

Passage IV · The last contact

Then the radar contact was gone

In the early evening of 10 November, Arthur M. Anderson was still in contact with the freighter. The last reported communication conveyed that the Fitzgerald was holding her own. At approximately 7:15 p.m. Eastern Standard Time, she disappeared. No distress call was received.

29 lives lost

All 29 officers and crew members aboard were lost. The absence of a distress transmission, together with the lack of survivors, leaves the final moments unknowable in important respects. Accounts describing precisely what happened on the bridge or below decks at the moment of loss go beyond the surviving direct evidence.

The wreck was located in deep water near Whitefish Bay, roughly 17 miles from its entrance. Later underwater examination documented major separated wreck sections. The condition of the wreck provides evidence, but interpreting the sequence of structural failure remains difficult.

Passage V · Competing explanations

What sank the Edmund Fitzgerald?

Investigators agreed that the ship suffered a catastrophic loss in severe weather, but differed in their explanation of how the flooding and final failure occurred.

United States Coast Guard

Flooding through ineffective hatch closures

The Coast Guard's marine casualty investigation identified loss of buoyancy and stability through massive cargo-hold flooding as the most probable explanation, with ineffective hatch closures implicated. Its report did not establish every detail conclusively.

Read the Coast Guard report (PDF)
National Transportation Safety Board

Sudden hatch-cover collapse

The NTSB concluded that sudden massive flooding of the cargo hold followed the collapse of one or more hatch covers. It also considered earlier flooding through other openings and damage affecting freeboard and stability.

Read the NTSB report (PDF)

Other interpretations have proposed grounding or structural failure. They belong in the history of the debate, but should not be elevated to established fact. No eyewitness survived from the ship to resolve the final sequence.

Interpretive restraint

The findings above are institutional conclusions based on available evidence, not a filmed or witnessed account of the final minutes. The exact initiating event and sequence of the sinking remain disputed.

Passage VI · After the loss

The people behind the name

The story of Edmund Fitzgerald is often told through the scale of the ship, the force of the storm, and the mystery of her disappearance. Its center, however, is the loss of 29 working mariners and the grief carried by their families, friends, and communities.

The disaster entered public memory through memorial services, maritime museums, and Gordon Lightfoot's 1976 song, The Wreck of the Edmund Fitzgerald. The song remains an important cultural remembrance, but it is not a substitute for the investigative record.

Remembrance

Twenty-nine, not merely a number

The Great Lakes Shipwreck Historical Society preserves the history of the loss and holds annual commemorations. Its materials also document the search and subsequent examination of the wreck.

Editorial note.

No invented last words, imagined final scenes, or unverified ship photographs are used. The exhibition makes no claim that the precise mechanism of loss has been conclusively established. The page's visual design follows the Ocean Liner Curator exhibition format.
